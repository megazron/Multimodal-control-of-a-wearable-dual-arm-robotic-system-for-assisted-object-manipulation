\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussionmain}

This project built and verified a teleoperation system for two arms worn by
one person and commanded by another, and piloted direct against
shared-autonomy control with real operators. Its principal findings are a
disjoint two-arm workspace with a costed and applied fix, a safety layer
whose live pilot record shows the geometric floor never engaged, and a pilot
in which assistance shortened sessions and reduced re-grips while
lengthening the robot's path.

\section{The workspace result, and what it costs}

The disjoint-workspace finding is severe because it is geometric: no
planner or orientation policy can produce a point neither arm can reach, so
inter-arm handover was excluded from the task set before anything else
excluded it. The cause is not the mount's rotation but the arms'
asymmetric home joint angles, ground truth because the sim-to-hardware
bridge replays them onto the real arm; fixing it needed a mechanical change,
costed, applied and re-verified as one pass, since every clearance and task
coordinate depends on it.

\section{The safety layer is thorough about distance and silent about time}

The clearance floor is a \emph{distance}, and the wearer can move. Combining
the measured wearer-tracking latency with literature limb-speed ranges gives
\cref{tab:reaction-compact}: in most modelled limb motions the limb travels
further than the entire \SI{150}{\milli\metre} margin before the perception
chain is aware anything changed -- the consequence of a fixed margin
meeting a half-second perception chain and a person who can move a metre
in that time. It is this
report's principal open safety question, and three responses exist, none
implemented: inflate the floor by latency times an assumed limb speed, a
one-parameter change that makes the margin mean what it claims; cut the
latency, since most of the \SI{562}{\milli\second} tail is a configured
publication rate limit, not the estimator, which runs at
\SIrange{28}{34}{\milli\second} per frame; and scale commanded speed with
measured clearance, two quantities the system already computes and keeps
apart. The fallback compounds it: with the wearer estimate stale or gated
out, the guard reverts to the arms-down mannequin and believes
\SI{0.3170}{\metre} of clearance where a wearer with arms out has
\SI{0.0933}{\metre} -- optimistic by \SI{224}{\milli\metre}, mitigated
today by an instruction to the wearer, not a control.

\section{Validity of the measurements}
\label{sec:validity}

Twenty-six times in this project a measurement was wrong and the system was
not (\cref{ch:instrument}), a base rate that conditions how every other
number here should be read. Fifteen of the twenty-six were caught by
an independently known quantity contradicting the reading, and fourteen
were already answerable from something in hand -- a published stroke, an
arithmetic prediction, a quantity exact by construction -- which is the
case for building the cross-check in rather than trusting harder. Every
result above except the pilot and channel measurements comes from
simulation with mock hardware, whose force channels are identically zero,
so no force-derived quantity is defined; and every latency reported is
control-path latency between software stages, so it is a floor.

\section{What the pilot shows, and its limits}
\label{sec:pilot-limits}

The pilot points the same direction as shared-autonomy literature developed
for robots attached to nobody~\cite{dragan2013policyblending,javdani2015hindsight}:
shorter completion times and fewer re-grips under assistance, with no
measurable cost in responsiveness. The robot's path is \emph{longer} under
shared autonomy, in the paired comparison too (six of seven cases) -- but
the operators' own controller paths are not: \SI{29.9}{\metre} against
\SI{30.5}{\metre}, within \SI{2}{\percent}. The extra distance is
the assistance layer's, not the operator's, and the platform shows the same
with no operator at all: identical scripted waypoints travel
\SI{4}{\percent} further under VR shared autonomy than direct VR, and
\SI{45}{\percent} further under master shared autonomy than master direct
(\repo{recordings/baselines/mode\_difference.json}). "Shorter but less
direct" is a property of the arbiter, not a contradiction. The literature
predicts the cost this pilot could not see: higher autonomy improves
performance but lowers sense of agency~\cite{collier2025agency}, autonomous
modes cut effort without raising satisfaction~\cite{kim2012autonomy}, and
assistance raises safety while lowering perceived
control~\cite{you2011assisted}; no agency or workload instrument was
administered here, which is why H2 is untested and H4 is in the protocol.
Five operators with uneven task coverage is a pilot-scale sample, not a
powered comparison; the paired comparison isolates the condition and rests
on seven cases. The full study's own pre-registered analysis, run on \num{20000}
constructed data sets (\cref{app:analysis}), recovers a
$d_z=\num{0.73}$ effect family-wise with \SI{97.3}{\percent} power at
sixteen dyads but on a \emph{named} task with only \SI{63.8}{\percent}
after correction; \SI{80}{\percent} on a named task needs twenty dyads,
which is the sample the next study should recruit. Most importantly, the
pilot has no separate wearer: it measures what
the operator experienced, not what carrying \SI{17}{\kilo\gram} of assisted
motion costs a second person, the comparison \cref{app:study} remains the
target for.

\section{Against the planning report}

The planning report specified physical springs at the master and virtual
impedance at the slave; neither survives the measured system, whose usable
command path runs at \SIrange{18.4}{18.7}{\hertz}, a rate at which an
impedance law is not one. The compliance sought would have to come from the
mechanism -- series elastic actuation~\cite{toubar2022sea,lee2019sea} or a
current-based formulation~\cite{dewolde2024impedance} -- both hardware
changes. The safety, degradation, workspace and pilot work were not in the
plan; each followed from a measurement it did not anticipate.

\section{Against the objectives, and what remains}
\label{sec:against-objectives}

Of the six objectives of \cref{sec:aim}, five were met and one partly: the
master built and characterised, its repair named to two joints (1); a
predictably degrading mapping (2); a safety layer that handled fourteen of fourteen injected
faults, every blocker observable, its live record showing the manual path
firing and the geometric path never needed (3); a workspace
characterisation that found no shared reachable point and costed, applied
and re-verified the fix (4); ten-repeat, known-answer verification that
caught twenty-six instrument defects before any became a finding (5); and a
pilot under approval that supports H1 directionally at pilot scale but,
with no workload instrument and no wearer, leaves H2--H5 untested, so
objective 6 is met in its first half only.

What remains, in priority order with the number that sets it: repair the
two named master channels (\SI{54.5}{\milli\metre} of unrequested motion);
calibrate the wrist-camera-to-arm transform (\SIrange{34}{59}{\milli\metre},
the largest unmeasured budget term and why no open-loop grasp has closed on
hardware); correct the clearance check that samples link origins rather
than geometry, the one safety defect here rather than incompleteness
(\cref{ch:discussion}); and run \cref{app:study}'s protocol with a separate
wearer, now that approval and an input path both exist.
