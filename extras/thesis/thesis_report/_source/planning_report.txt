1. Project Specification 
Wearable supernumerary robotic limbs (SRLs) augment human capabilities and assist individuals 
with limited mobility by providing extra manipulation arms that move synchronously with the user [1, 
4]. The MUlti -limb Virtual Environment (MUVE), known as the Dr. Octopus platform at Imperial 
College London, mounts up to dual lightweight Kinova 7-DoF robotic arms on a backpack. This enables 
coordinated object manipulation in daily environments while supporting multisensory integration [2]. 
The overall goal of the project is to enable safe and efficient control of up to dual supernumerary 
robotic arms using the fingers of one or two hands moving on a small physical mannequin. The 
mannequin acts as the master device, directly commanding the dual full-scale Kinova arms (the slaves) 
mounted on the Dr. Octopus backpack. The project is structured in three sequential stages: Stage 1 
consists of physical prototyping with a figurine and real springs to determine optimal spring placement 
for intuitive force feedback together with simulation setup of the Kinova arm dynamics in Isaac Lab to 
validate virtual spring behaviour; Stage 2 comprises the complete simulation in Issac Lab along with 
design of the physical mannequin master hardware with real miniature arms and the design of the master-
to-slave control system for the dual Kinova arms; Stage 3 involves implementation of representative 
tasks like pick and place and their demonstration. This project specifically focuses on  the master 
mannequin design and control mapping for the master-slave manipulator. Using the physical mannequin 
as the master device makes the overall teleoperation system intrinsically safe through direct finger 
interaction; and finally, the multimodal vision-based object detection and gesture-based intent interfaces 
completed previously by the team will be integrated where relevant. 
The Kinova Gen3 arms support admittance and impedance control modes via the Kortex API, 
enabling virtual spring -damper dynamics that emulate the compliance of physical Series Elastic 
Actuators (SEAs) [3, 10]. A Series Elastic Actuator (SEA) places an elastic element, such as a spring, 
in series between a motor/gearbox and the output load. This design enables precise force control, 
increased shock tolerance, and safe, compliant interaction with humans, making  them ideal for 
exoskeletons, prosthetics, and walking robots [9, 13]. 
The project delivers a closed-loop haptic control demonstration for coordinated object manipulation 
using the dual Kinova arms, with virtual springs providing intuitive force feedback. 
Aims: To design, prototype, and implement a master -slave teleoperation system using a physical 
figurine mannequin with miniature arms that is controlled by the fingers of one or two hands, thereby 
enabling safe and efficient intuitive control of up to dual Kinova supernumerary robotic limbs on the Dr. 
Octopus platform. 
Objectives: The objectives of this project are to conduct physical prototyping with a figurine and 
real springs to determine optimal spring placement for intuitive force feedback and to perform simulation 
of the Kinova arm dynamics in Isaac Lab or MuJoCo to validate virtual spring behaviour, to design the 
physical mannequin master hardware with real miniature motorised arms and to design the control 
mapping that commands the dual Kinova 7 -DoF slave arms while ensuring stable master -slave 
synchronisation and haptic transparency, and to implement representative tasks such as coordinated 
pick-and-place operations and synchronised multi -arm motions and demonstrate them on the real Dr. 
Octopus platform after completion of the master hardware and control design. B oth the master 
mannequin design and the slave control mapping will be developed in parallel to accelerate integration. 
This work advances SRL teleoperation by bridging physical miniature prototyping with haptic springs 
to full-scale wearable hardware on the Dr. Octopus platform [1, 6].

2. Ethical Analysis 
This project is laboratory-based and involves only robotic hardware development and testing. No 
human subjects beyond voluntary, supervised researcher demonstrations (with informed consent) are 
required. No biological specimens or personal data are collected. The only human involvement consists 
of voluntary, supervised researcher demonstrations and a small user study of 8–10 laboratory volunteers, 
all of whom will provide written informed consent prior to participation and retain the right to withdraw 
at any time without consequence. Testing complies with Imperial College robotic safety protocols. 
Risks are confined to minor mechanical hazards during arm motion, mitigated by Kinova safety 
features (joint limits, emergency stops, impedance-based compliance) and supervised operation. Long-
term benefits include safer, more intuitive assistive robotics for mobility-impaired users and industrial 
augmentation, with negligible environmental impact through reuse of existing Kinova hardware.  The 
work follows responsible robotics principles emphasising safety and human-centred design.  
At the hardware level, the physical mannequin serves a dual purpose beyond control by interacting 
with the miniature figurine during development, the operator naturally becomes aware of geometrically 
possible contacts between the four full -scale Kinova arms and the human body. This embodied 
awareness informs safer arm trajectory design b efore any live testing occurs, effectively making the 
prototyping process itself a safety validation tool. All mechanical components, including 3D -printed 
parts, coil springs, and servo motors, are selected and assembled to eliminate sharp edges, pinch points, 
and failure modes that could cause injury during handling. 
At the electronics level, all wiring, connectors, and power supplies operate within low -voltage 
safety thresholds. Current -limiting protections are implemented in hardware to prevent motor 
overcurrent during unexpected joint collisions, and all electronic enclosures are secured to prevent 
accidental contact during operation. 
At the software level, the Kinova Kortex API is operated exclusively within manufacturer-specified 
joint torque and velocity limits. Impedance control parameters are first validated in simulation before 
deployment on physical hardware, ensuring no unsafe g ain combinations reach the real system. 
Redundant emergency stop functionality is implemented in software alongside the physical E -stop 
buttons, with automatic velocity zeroing triggered upon communication loss between master and slave. 
From a human -centred design perspective, the mannequin interaction paradigm inherently keeps 
the operator physically engaged with the system's geometry throughout development, maintaining 
continuous awareness of reachable body regions and promoting intuiti ve safe operation. The work 
therefore follows responsible robotics principles at every stage, embedding safety not as an afterthought 
but as a structural property of the design process itself. 
The environmental footprint of this project is deliberately minimised. All Kinova Gen3 hardware 
is reused from existing laboratory inventory, avoiding the carbon and material cost of new 
manufacturing. Miniature arm components are fabricated using desktop 3D printing, with recyclable 
filament materials used wherever available. Most of the Electronic components will be sourced from 
existing laboratory stocks.

3. Literature Review 
Supernumerary robotic limbs (SRLs) have emerged as a key technology for human augmentation, 
drawing interest from both assistive technology and industrial domains. Comprehensive reviews 
quantify their design parameters, actuation mechanisms, intent recogni tion strategies, and multimodal 
feedback approaches across a range of applications [1, 4]. The Dr. Octopus platform at Imperial College 
London exemplifies a wearable multi -arm system capable of supporting physical interaction in both 
virtual and real environments, mounting up to dual Kinova 7-DoF arms on a backpack frame [2]. Early 
SRL prototypes focused primarily on passive augmentation for specific tasks such as overhead assembly; 
subsequent systems incorporated active impedance control to improve safety and naturalness of 
interaction [4, 8]. 
Master-slave teleoperation has been widely adopted in redundant manipulators and SRLs to provide 
intuitive, low -latency control [7]. Bilateral teleoperation schemes, in which force and position 
information flow in both directions between master and slave, are considered essential for safe physical 
interaction and transparent haptic feedback [10]. Recent work on Kinova Gen3 teleoperation 
demonstrates effective mapping from human gestures or physical master devices to 7 -DoF slave arms, 
highlighting the importance of current-based impedance control for interacting with mobile manipulators 
[10]. Physical prototyping with miniature models is a well -established approach to validate haptic 
feedback and control mapping before scaling to full hardware [12]. Guggenhei m and Asada 
demonstrated that inherent haptic feedback from SRL limbs can meaningfully reduce cognitive load 
during teleoperation, motivating the use of spring -based compliance at the master stage [12]. 
Embodiment studies in virtual reality further show that physical congruence between the master device 
and the robot morphology enhances the user’s sense of agency over supernumerary limbs [8]. 
Haptic feedback is essential for closed-loop teleoperation and embodiment in SRLs. Series Elastic 
Actuators (SEAs) place a compliant element in series between the actuator and the load, enabling precise 
force control, shock tolerance, and safe human intera ction; their principles directly inform the virtual 
spring design used in this project [9, 13]. Physical springs on the master device, as in the figurine 
prototyping stage, provide direct mechanical force reflection, while virtual springs implemented through 
impedance control in the Kinova slave arms replicate this compliance at scale [3, 9]. Pinardi et al. 
demonstrated that supplementary sensory feedback significantly improves the control quality and 
embodiment of SRLs during manipulation tasks, underscori ng the value of the haptic channel in this 
system [14]. Simulation tools such as Isaac Lab enable safe offline tuning of impedance parameters and 
master-slave mapping before deployment on real hardware, reducing the risk of hardware damage during 
early int egration [11]. Spatial considerations for near -body SRL interaction including collision 
avoidance and ergonomic placement have also been studied to ensure the dual-arm configuration 
remains practical during daily tasks [15].

Literature reveals a gap: few studies combine a custom miniature dual-arm master mannequin (with 
physical springs) and direct teleoperation of a wearable dual-arm SRL platform for coordinated 
manipulation tasks [1, 6]. Existing multi-arm SRL studies either rely on gestural or VR-based interfaces 
with higher cognitive load, or use single-arm master-slave configurations that do not scale to dual-limb 
coordination [4, 7]. Moreover, prior work rarely integrates SEA -style physical compliance at the 
miniature master stage with virtual spring impedance at the full -scale slave stage simultaneously; this 
dual-layer compliance strategy is a distinguishing feature of the present project [9, 12]. This project 
addresses the identified gap through a three-stage process of physical prototyping, parallel master-and-
control design, and full-system task demonstration on the Dr. Octopus platform.

4. Implementation Plan 
Work completed to date has focused on Stage 1 of the project. Physical prototyping has been carried 
out by placing real springs on a figurine model to determine optimal attachment points for intuitive force 
feedback, and simulation and design setup of the Kinova 7-DoF arm dynamics has begun in Isaac Lab 
to validate virtual spring behaviour. 
The remaining work will proceed with Stage 2 and Stage 3. In Stage 2, which will run in parallel, 
Simulation of the dual arms in Isaac Lab the physical mannequin master hardware with real miniature 
motorised arms will be designed and constructed while the master-to-slave control mapping for the dual 
Kinova slave arms is simultaneously developed and simulated. In Stage 3, following completion of the 
master hardware and control design, representative tasks will be implemented on the real Dr. Octopus 
platform and demonstrated; these tasks include coordinated pick-and-place operations and synchronised 
multi-arm motions such as dancing sequences. Full system integration, safety validation, laboratory 
testing, performance tuning, documentation, and preparation of the final demonstration video will 
conclude the project. Key milestones include validation of virtual spring parameters and completion of 
the basic master mannequin and control mapping by the end of May 2026, operational master -slave 
synchronisation on two arms by the end of June, full dual-arm teleoperation and task demonstrations by 
the end of July, and complete evaluation by the end of August. Fallback positions have been identified: 
if miniature arm fabrication delays occur, a simplified 2-DoF prototype per arm will be used for initial 
mapping tests; if real-time slave synchronisation proves unstable, conservative scaling factors validated 
on the physical figurine will be applied before full deployment.

6. Evaluation 
Success of the project will be measured through a combination of quantitative performance metrics 
and qualitative user feedback. Master-slave tracking accuracy will be assessed by comparing miniature 
master joint angles to slave end-effector positions, targeting positional errors below 10 cm and angular 
errors below 15 degrees. Haptic transparency will be verified by ensuring force reflection errors remain 
below 0.8 N via the physical springs on the mannequin and Kinova torque feedback. Task-level metrics 
will include coordinated pick -and-place success rate above 80 percent and average completion time 
under 45 seconds per object, as well as synchronised motion accuracy for dancing demonstrations 
(measured by phase lag below 200 ms across arms). Closed -loop s tability will be confirmed by 
oscillations below 8 percent overshoot and end -to-end latency under 200 ms. In addition, a small user 
study involving 8-10 laboratory volunteers will gather qualitative impressions of haptic intuitiveness and 
embodiment when controlling the dual arms via finger movements on the mannequin. Experiments will 
use standardised household objects in a controlled lab arena, comparing baseline position control against 
the full master -slave system with haptic feedback. Simple statistical comparisons (e.g.,  average and 
standard deviation) will be used to evaluate differences between conditions.

Figure 1: Spring attachment configurations on the Kinova Gen3 7-DoF arm figurine (J1–J7). (A) Full 
connectivity: all geometrically feasible inter -joint spring connections, providing maximum force 
information at the cost of coupled, ambiguous signals. (B) Informative subset: connections retained 
based on single-joint deflection dominance, eliminating cross-coupled springs that respond to multiple 
degrees of freedom simultaneously. (C) Optimal configuration: the minimal spring set that satisfies 
both postural observability and signal separability, enabling the operator to infer arm configuration 
comfort and joint proximity through clean, independently interpretable haptic feedback at the master 
fingertips. 
 
To determine optimal spring placement on the miniature figurine master, three configurations of inter -
joint spring connections were systematically evaluated across the seven joints (J1 –J7) of the Kinova 
Gen3 arm morphology, as shown in Figure 1. For seven joints, the maximum number of 21 unique 
pairwise connections. 
Configuration A retains a representative subset of these, connecting non-adjacent joints across the 
kinematic chain. While this maximises force information richness, it introduces mechanical coupling 
ambiguity the contribution of individual joint displacements to spring deformation cannot be cleanly 
isolated, making signal attribution unreliable.

Configuration B retains only connections that produce mechanically meaningful and attributable 
spring deflection signals. Specifically, a connection is kept only if its deformation is dominated by the 
motion of a single joint, rather than being spread across multiple joints simultaneously. This eliminates 
cross-coupled connections whose force signals would be ambiguous at the operator's fingertips, leaving 
a reduced set of springs each acting as a clean proprioceptive channel for one dominant degree of 
freedom. In the Fig ure 2. The figurine also has a support spring which separates the upper arms with 
lower arms.

Configuration C represents the optimal minimal set identified through this process. It provides 
sufficient coverage to infer the global postural state of the arm including whether a given configuration 
is ergonomically comfortable or approaching a joint limit while keeping the haptic signals at the master 
fingertips clean and separable. Each spring in Configuration C responds predominantly to one degree of 
freedom: the J5–J6 connection encodes elbow flexion state, J3–J2 encodes wrist proximity to the base, 
and J4–J1 encodes overall arm extension relative to the base. Together, these three connections form a 
minimal observability basis for the seven -DoF arm posture, providing the operator with intuitive 
directional feedback about which part of the arm is under strain withou t requiring them to interpret 
overlapping or competing force signals. Configuration C also represents the virtual springs present in 
Kinova Gen3 Arm, Hence C would be the best approach to add on physical figurine. 
 
 
Figure 4: CAD model of the miniature master mannequin arm assembly shown from three 
perspectives: (A) back view, (B) front view, and (C) isometric view. The model comprises a scaled -
down kinematic replica of the Kinova Gen3 7 -DoF arm mounted on a symmetric cross -frame base, 
with coiled spring elements positioned along the arm links to provide haptic force feedback to the 
operator's fingertips. 
The arm structure follows the seven-degree-of-freedom kinematic chain of the Kinova Gen3 arm, 
scaled down to a size operable by human fingers. Coiled spring elements are integrated along the arm 
links at the joint locations identified as optimal in the Con figuration C Spring placement study. These 
springs provide the physical compliance that reflects the postural state of the arm back to the operator 
as a tangible haptic force, without requiring any electronics or sensing at the master stage. The isometric 
view (C) most clearly illustrates the three-dimensional spatial arrangement of the joints and the relative 
positioning of the spring elements along the kinematic chain, confirming that the design captures the full 
workspace geometry of the Kinova arm in miniature form.
