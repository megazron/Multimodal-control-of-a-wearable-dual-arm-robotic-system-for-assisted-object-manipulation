\section{Scope of the evidence, and how to read this chapter}

Every result in this chapter is labelled with the strength of evidence behind
it, using three tiers:

\begin{description}
  \item[Simulated] measured against a running planning and control stack with
    a mock hardware interface;
  \item[Mock-exercised] the code path ran and its failure modes were injected,
    but no physical device was involved;
  \item[Hardware] a physical arm, a physical microcontroller or a human
    participant took part.
\end{description}

\textbf{No result in this chapter is of the Hardware tier.} No experiment was
run with a human participant, and no logged artefact from a physical arm
exists in the project repository. The engineering log records real-arm
bring-up activity, but as no corresponding data file was retained, those
figures are not reported here as measurements of this work.

\section{Workspace and reachability}

\subsection{Continuous reachability from the home pose}

Reachability was measured as a swept extent rather than as pointwise
feasibility, because teleoperation requires the arm to travel to a pose from
where it already is, without a branch change. Twenty-six directions were swept
in one-centimetre steps from the home configuration, each step seeded from the
previous solution.

Repeating the sweep ten times gave identical quartiles: the inter-quartile
range was $0.000$~m on every direction for both arms. The measurement is
therefore repeatable. What was \emph{not} repeatable in earlier runs was the
summary statistic: isotropy computed as the minimum over directions of a
single sweep is dominated by rare early terminations, and varied by a factor
of two between runs. On medians it is stable, at $0.16$ for the left arm and
$0.10$ for the right. \emph{(Simulated;
\texttt{recordings/baselines/workspace\_n10\_20260806.json}.)}

The early terminations were traced to the measuring tool rather than the
system. A sweep is a chain of up to ninety solver calls and any single failure
ends the direction; the follower does not suffer this because it re-seeds the
redundant joint and retries. Giving the sweep the same retry reduced early
terminations from nine to zero on the left arm and three to zero on the right,
while the medians barely moved --- which is what confirms the outliers were
chain compounding rather than geometry.

\subsection{The two arms share no workspace}

Over a grid of sixty-three cells spanning the frontal working volume, no cell
was reachable by both arms. With collision checking disabled only three cells
were shared, and the wearer's own geometry removed all three.
\emph{(Simulated; \texttt{scripts/verify\_task\_scenes.py}.)}

This is a geometric property of the mount and the parked joint configuration,
not a planning or control limitation, and it is not addressable by a wearer
reposition, because moving the base moves both arms together and leaves their
mutual separation unchanged. The consequence is that any task requiring the
two arms to meet at a point --- an inter-arm handover --- cannot be performed
in this configuration.

A parametric sweep over candidate mount placements indicated that a buildable
change, reducing the separation of the two mounts, would raise the number of
shared cells from three to twenty-two.
\emph{(Simulated; \texttt{recordings/baselines/mount\_overlap\_sweep.json}.)}
The sweep is purely kinematic: it moves the arms' bases without moving the
wearer, so its figures are an upper bound and any candidate requires a full
clearance check before it is adopted. It was recorded and not applied, because
changing the mount invalidates the home pose, the workspace anchor and every
clearance figure in this chapter.

\section{Master-arm channel health}

Fourteen potentiometer channels were classified from a recorded capture. The
classification distinguishes four states, of which one had to be introduced
during the analysis: a failing channel does not fall silent, but returns
values spanning a plausible range that are not a trajectory, with consecutive
updates unrelated. Range alone calls that healthy.

Six of the fourteen channels were usable.
\emph{(Simulated analysis of recorded data;
\texttt{recordings/baselines/channels\_20260806.json}.)}

Two prior conclusions were overturned by this analysis, both because the
recorder sampled at approximately $50$~Hz while the sensors update at
$14.7$--$17$~Hz, so $82$--$88$\% of adjacent rows are bit-identical. Any
statistic computed over consecutive rows is therefore dominated by duplicates:
the apparent noise floor of $0.000$ on every channel is an artefact of the
recorder, and the zero-spread signature previously used to identify a dead
channel is the same artefact. Computed between distinct sensor updates, the
noise floor is $0.2$--$5.4^\circ$.

\subsection{Cost of degraded operation}

With the incoherent channels frozen at their reference values, the
contribution of those channels alone to the commanded tip position was
measured by replaying recorded traces with every coherent channel held at its
median. The left arm's incoherent channels contributed $54.5$~mm of commanded
tip motion from a perfectly still hand; the right arm's contributed
$0.014$~mm. \emph{(Simulated replay of recorded data;
\texttt{scripts/prove\_degraded\_mode.py}.)}

Regressing the true reach on the surviving channels showed that the left arm
retains no reach observable at all once its shoulder and elbow channels are
excluded ($R^2 = 0.133$, residual $93$\% of the true spread), whereas the
right arm does ($R^2 = 0.986$). This identifies the two specific channels
whose repair would restore the most capability.

\section{Safety layer behaviour}

\subsection{Fault injection}

Fourteen distinct faults were injected and all fourteen were handled, meaning
the fault was detected, the system responded in the designed way, and the
response was visible in the published state.
\emph{(Mock-exercised; \texttt{recordings/fault\_acceptance/}.)}

Faults were also injected mid-trial into all six experiment runners. In each
case the affected trial was marked invalid with the cause recorded, the
session continued, and the analysis excluded the trial as pre-registered.
A session in which every trial is invalidated fails loudly rather than
analysing to an empty result, which previously was indistinguishable from a
null finding.

\subsection{The dead-man defect}

The most consequential defect found in the safety layer was that the dead-man
could not detect the failure that actually occurs. The master node does not
fall silent when its input degrades: it substitutes its last good value and
continues publishing at full rate. Because it stamped that substituted message
with the current time, neither the topic nor the timestamp went stale, and a
dead-man keyed on message arrival could not fire.

Only a clean cable removal produced silence, which is why every cable-pull test
had passed. The test and the failure mode were different failures. After
keying the dead-man on the source timestamp of the data, the trip times
measured were $0.94$--$0.99$~s for a degraded-but-publishing master and
$0.97$~s for a silent one, against a deadline of $1.00$~s, with no false
positive over $243$ fresh frames. \emph{(Mock-exercised.)}

\section{Recorded verification of the task set}

Every task, scenario and autonomy condition was driven in simulation and
recorded, giving eighty-seven runs. Each was checked automatically for
content, for object handling and for gripper actuation.

Across all eighty-seven runs, no arm link entered the wearer's collision
geometry, and no run showed a stationary robot.

\subsection{Objects are carried, not merely rendered}

The check that the manipulated object travels with the gripper was made from
the recorded video rather than from the code that drew it, on the grounds that
the marker pose is computed from the live end-effector pose and would
therefore track by construction. For the pick-and-place task the evidence
requires no centroid tracking at all: the count of pixels belonging to the
supply of blocks falls as blocks are removed, while the count belonging to
placed blocks inside the container rises. A gripper closing on nothing changes
neither.

\subsection{A defect found by watching, not by testing}

The gripper was never commanded in the first round of recordings. The mock
hardware interface initialises the driven knuckle joint at a fully closed
value, and because no component in the recording path issued a gripper
command, the hand remained closed for the entire duration of every clip while
the grasp was represented only by the attached marker.

This was not caught by the object-attachment check, which passed: the marker
genuinely was following the gripper. It was following a gripper that never
opened. \emph{(Simulated.)} The recording path now commands the gripper
controller directly, closing to a width derived from each object's dimensions
rather than to full closure, and the marker attaches only while the measured
joint indicates closure on an object.

\section{Clearance: a contact test is not a margin}

Driving every task and measuring clearance to the wearer produced the result
that has the largest consequence for the experimental programme. Every
scenario in the chest-height working band passes inverse kinematics with
collision checking enabled --- the planner reports the poses valid --- and yet
the arms pass within $48$--$67$~mm of the wearer's torso, which is inside the
$120$~mm margin the real-robot configuration enforces.
\emph{(Simulated; per-run \texttt{summary.json}.)}

The reconciliation is that the two tests ask different questions. The
planner's check is binary contact; the floor is a margin. A scenario can
satisfy one and be refused by the other, and this set of scenarios does.

A dedicated probe established three things about the cause. The closest link
is the wrist rather than the forearm, which indicates that the targets are too
close to the torso rather than that the path routes badly. Moving the working
band forward does not help: at a forward offset that clears the floor, only one
of six probed poses remains reachable, and beyond that nothing is reachable at
all --- clearance and reach are in direct conflict along that axis. Moving the
band outboard does work: increasing the lateral half-span from $155$~mm to
$250$~mm gives $134$~mm of clearance with all six poses still reachable, and
every value tested beyond that also clears.
\emph{(Simulated;
\texttt{scripts/probe\_task\_band\_clearance.py}.)}

The corroboration is independent: the two tasks that already work at a lateral
half-span of $400$~mm measured $158$--$187$~mm of clearance across thirty
runs, with none below the floor.

The change was recorded and not applied, because a $250$~mm half-span implies
a $500$~mm object, which exceeds the retention threshold of the compliant
carrier used in one task and rescales the tilt threshold of another.

\section{Verified task scenarios}

All seventeen scenario definitions, and all seventeen coordinates written in
the task protocols, were verified at ten repeats over paths densified to
$20$~mm, and all pass.
\emph{(Simulated; \texttt{recordings/baselines/scenario\_audit.yaml}.)}

Reaching that state required correcting figures that had been established by
weaker checks. Eleven of the fourteen coordinates originally written into the
protocol documents were reachable by no arm at all, being from a superseded
table-height layout. One task's documented hold and release poses were wrong
in every particular, including the assignment of arms.

The specific failure mode is worth stating because it recurs: a pose at the
edge of the feasible set passes intermittently. One pose measured $72$--$82$\%
feasible per call, which passes a five-repeat check roughly one time in five.
