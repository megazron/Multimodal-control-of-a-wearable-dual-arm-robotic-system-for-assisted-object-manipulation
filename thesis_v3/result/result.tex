\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:resultsmain}

Every number below carries its strength of evidence, and \textbf{no result
before the pilot in \cref{sec:pilot-results} was validated on a physical
Kinova arm carrying a load}: most of this project is simulated against mock
hardware, plus recorded data from the physical master and four cameras
(\cref{ch:results}: full derivations).

\section{The command mapping and its safety layer}
\label{sec:safety}

Six of fourteen master channels are alive (\cref{sec:channelhealth}; the
degradation architecture is discussed in \cref{sec:degradation-appendix}).

The safety layer handled fourteen of fourteen injected faults; the two that needed several attempts both traced to a
mechanism keyed on the wrong observable, fixed by reading the master data's
source timestamp rather than its arrival, and by parking the arm before
talking to the driver.

The pilot (\cref{sec:pilot-results}) is the only \emph{live} evidence for
this layer (\cref{fig:estop-timeline}): across every recorded session the
geometric clearance floor -- the mechanism that would hold an arm
\SI{150}{\milli\metre} short of the wearer -- never activated once. The
manual emergency stop
did fire: twelve holds across eleven of twenty-three sessions, nine of them
in a session's final \SI{15}{\percent}, the operator ending the recording,
and three not -- one assisted session tripped at \SI{67}{\percent} and
\SI{83}{\percent} of its length with steering resuming after each. The
geometric path remains validated only against synthetic fault injection.

\section{Workspace and motion}

Over a 63-cell grid spanning the frontal working volume, \textbf{no cell was
reachable by both arms} (\cref{fig:disjoint}), and the result survives
freeing the wrist yaw and adding solver retries.

  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\linewidth}
    \centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{mount_sweep.pdf}
    \caption{the costed fix}
    \label{fig:disjoint-b}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption[The disjoint workspace, and the mount change that fixes it]{
  (\subref{fig:disjoint-a}) The two reachable sets do not meet.
  (\subref{fig:disjoint-b}) A mount-placement sweep: forward tilt and
  inboard translation help, splaying outward \emph{hurts}. Kinematic only,
  so these are upper bounds pending a clearance check.}
  \label{fig:disjoint}
\end{figure}

Moving the mounts inboard, forward and down with more forward tilt raises
shared cells to 22 of 63 within a buildable \SI{0.20}{\metre} separation;
applied, it raised home-pose clearance from \SI{0.1610}{} to
\SI{0.2202}{\metre} and moved pick-and-place targets from \SI{0.530}{} to
\SI{0.290}{\metre} off centre. What binds hardest is
the pinned wrist: from the work point, mean reach is \SI{0.065}{\metre}
with the wrist pinned against \SI{0.363}{\metre} free, over five times,
because a fixed pose spends the seventh joint -- the one that moves the
elbow out of the person (\cref{tab:work-point}).

The per-joint step limiter that was the whole motion generator is compared
with its jerk-limited replacement in \cref{tab:motiongen-main}: it left the
hand off the solved path by nearly twice the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} capture
gate, at over twelve times the declared joint speed, because it never read
the limits file.

  \caption[Motion generator comparison]{The same commanded slew, both

  distance the hand leaves the solver's straight line.}

    \toprule

Replaying recorded joint commands on the physical arm shows every joint
parks \SI{0.305}{\degree} short of its target on the side it approached
from -- a terminal offset, not a gain, by Jacobian propagation
($r=\num{0.923}$). One scalar per arm removes \SIrange{74}{76}{\percent} of
it, taking the positioning budget from \SI{18.64}{} to
\SI{12.91}{\milli\metre} RSS (\SI{33.39}{} to \SI{19.99}{\milli\metre}
worst), inside the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} requirement \emph{provided} the
unmeasured camera-to-wrist transform is small. Measured on the physical
arm, it is not: \SI{8.45}{\degree}, \SIrange{34}{59}{\milli\metre} over the
working range (\cref{sec:hardware-session}).

\section{Perception: what the cameras actually detected}

Pointed at four physical cameras for the first time (\cref{ch:vision}), the
wrist cameras fit a
support plane by random sample consensus~\cite{fischler1981ransac} at
\SI{1.43}{\milli\metre} RMS with \SI{74}{\percent} inliers
at working distance -- within a factor of three of the same code against a
renderer. Segmentation is scoped to the task's two objects
(\cref{fig:vision-pipeline}): the cube by the project's own colour
classifier, the box -- outside that palette -- by a threshold on its
measured hue; both recover as oriented boxes, which is what makes a turned
object's true width available to the planner.

No grasp closed on any camera this session: the nearest object's narrowest
axis measured \SI{144}{\milli\metre} against an \SI{85}{\milli\metre} jaw.
\Cref{fig:scene-boxes-full} places the scene against the safety layer's

In simulation, the full task set solves \num{13927} inverse-kinematics
calls with zero failures and zero clearance breaches, and every scripted
grasp closes on its object in every mode (\cref{fig:grasp-grid}); the cubes
are set down \SI{56}{\milli\metre} from their pad centre, past the
\SI{30}{\milli\metre} gate (\cref{fig:pick-frames}). Sweeping the
shared-autonomy intent estimator against a mis-declared table shows why:
from declared coordinates it is \emph{confidently wrong} \SI{35}{\percent}
of the time at one object pitch (\SI{60}{\milli\metre}) and already
\SI{17.5}{\percent} at \SI{40}{\milli\metre} -- past the \SI{16}{\percent}
error rate above which Dragan and Srinivasa found aggressive assistance a
net loss~\cite{dragan2012formalizing}; from measured coordinates it stays
at \SI{0.0}{\percent} across the sweep.

\section{A pilot of direct control against shared autonomy}
\label{sec:pilot-results}

\textbf{Five operators (P1--P5)} completed twenty-three recorded sessions
under the protocol of \cref{sec:ethics}, each on one of three VR-teleoperated
tasks -- \emph{object tracking} (following a moving held object with the
end-effector), \emph{target reaching} (a discrete reach to a marked
position) and \emph{position matching} (driving to and holding a target
pose) -- once direct, once with shared-autonomy assistance.
P5's seven unlabelled sessions are pooled, their condition read from the
logged flag. A fourth, planned task using a
multimeter as the manipulated object was never recorded with a participant
on any of three tries (\cref{app:pilot}, which also carries every session
and the coverage table; \cref{app:gallery} shows every session). Coverage
is uneven, so the pooled comparison mixes tasks and the paired one isolates
the condition.

Pooled across all twenty-three sessions (\cref{tab:pilot-summary},
\cref{fig:pilot-time-a}), shared-autonomy sessions averaged shorter
completion times (\SI{219}{\second} against \SI{241}{\second}) and fewer
re-grips per session (\num{23.3} against \num{25.8}), with a robot lag under
\SI{2.9}{\milli\metre} in both conditions -- but a \emph{longer} robot
end-effector path (\SI{2.78}{\metre} against \SI{2.30}{\metre}), a direction
that holds in the paired comparison too (\cref{fig:pilot-variance},
\cref{sec:pilot-limits}). \Cref{fig:paired-slopes} isolates
the seven cases in which the \emph{same} operator repeated a task under both
conditions: the robot travels further with assistance in six of seven, the
operator's own hand in only two of seven.
