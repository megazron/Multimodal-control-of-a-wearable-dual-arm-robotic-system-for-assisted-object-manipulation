\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This report documents a wearable supernumerary robotic limb system built over
six weeks: two Kinova Gen3 seven-degree-of-freedom manipulators mounted on a
backpack frame, worn by one person and driven by a second person through an
instrumented mannequin master arm, a virtual-reality motion-capture path, or a
typed or spoken natural-language goal. Around those three input routes sits a
shared-autonomy layer, a perception stack that measures the working
environment rather than being told about it, and a safety architecture whose
central object is the body of the person wearing the machine.

The engineering content is substantial --- sixteen ROS 2 packages, about
164\,000 lines of Python, 114 test modules, 233 analysis and verification
scripts, 71 design and findings documents, all catalogued program by program in
the appendices --- but the more transferable content is methodological. The project's governing rule is that
\emph{a surprising measurement is evidence about the instrument until the
instrument has been cleared}, and that rule has been correct seventeen
separate times. Chapters 12 and 13 catalogue the instruments that produced
confident wrong answers: a clearance model that returned the same number for
an arm inside a mannequin and an arm in free space; a status topic that
republished a frozen snapshot with fresh timestamps; a test gate that could
invent a failing test out of a string literal; a render check that filed a
scene containing nothing as passing because the wearer occupied 18\,\% of the
frame.

Chapter~17 is the complete register of that work: \textbf{265 numbered
faults}, in the order they were found, each with its symptom, its mechanism and
its resolution. Chapter~18 is the corresponding change log --- all 392 commits,
oldest first, with what each one changed and why.

The system's present capability is stated without hedging. It can measure a
table and enumerate what stands on it to sub-millimetre accuracy; it can plan
a grasp and refuse an unsafe one by name; it can execute a four-cube
colour-matched pick-and-place on both arms from a typed English sentence; and
it maps a volume with both arms and plans against the map. It cannot yet place
a hand inside the 30\,mm grasp capture gate from an open-loop plan --- the
measured worst-case positioning error is 33.4\,mm and one term of the budget
remains unmeasured --- and its wearer guard cannot react to a person who moves
quickly, because in five of six modelled limb motions the limb travels further
than the entire 150\,mm clearance floor before the guard hears about it. No
human data has been collected; nothing has been worn.

\chapter{Introduction and scope}

\section{The system in one paragraph}

Two Kinova Gen3 seven-degree-of-freedom arms are mounted on a backpack frame
worn on a person's back. A second person drives them. The driving input can be
an instrumented mannequin arm --- a physical linkage with a potentiometer on
each joint and an inertial measurement unit at the wrist, read by a Teensy 4.1
over serial --- or a pair of Meta Quest controllers held as six-degree-of-freedom
motion capture, or a sentence typed or spoken in English. Between the input
and the metal sits a shared-autonomy layer that can supply wrist orientation,
infer a goal, arbitrate a handover, or take the task over entirely. The whole
stack is ROS 2 Jazzy on Ubuntu under WSL2, with Fast~DDS restricted to shared
memory transport.

\keyfact{\textbf{Two people, and this is structural.} The person wearing the
arms and the person driving them are different people. Both are participants;
both consent separately; the wearer has their own outcome measures. The wearer
is not part of the apparatus, and the wearer's body is in the robot's
collision model. Every safety argument in this report follows from that one
sentence.}

\section{What this report is}

It is a complete technical account of the work to 26 August 2026, written as a
standalone document, in four parts:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Chapters 1--16, the narrative.} What the system is, how each
      subsystem works, what was measured, and what it is honestly capable of.
\item \textbf{Chapter 17, every failure.} The defect register: 265 numbered
      faults in the order they were found, with the mechanism named in each.
\item \textbf{Chapter 18, every change.} All 392 commits, oldest first, each
      with its own account of what it changed.
\item \textbf{Appendices A--C, every program.} Every module, script and test in
      the repository, with its size and its own description, followed by the
      baseline artefacts, the hard constraints and a glossary.
\end{itemize}
 It does not update, replace or summarise the existing
thesis draft in \file{thesis\_report/}; it is a separate synthesis, assembled
by reading the source tree, the test suite, the recorded baselines and the
7\,099-line diagnostic history in \file{docs/system/findings.md}.

Three conventions are used throughout.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Every number is a measurement or it says it is not.} Where a
      quantity has never been measured, this report writes UNMEASURED rather
      than an estimate. A budget with a guessed term hides the term you should
      go and measure.
\item \textbf{Defects are reported with their mechanism.} ``The check was
      wrong'' is not useful; ``the check compared a constant that includes the
      immobile mount stub against a floor that constant can never violate'' is.
\item \textbf{Claims are attributed to the artefact that establishes them.}
      Where a claim rests on a script, a test or a recorded baseline, that
      file is named.
\end{itemize}

\section{How the work was done}

The project has one methodological rule, stated in the repository's own
standing instructions and enforced by the test suite:

\keyfact{\textbf{Before reporting a bad measurement, validate the measuring
tool against ground truth.} A surprising failure is evidence about the
instrument until the instrument has been cleared. This has been the fault
seventeen times.}

Four corollaries have accumulated around it, each from a specific incident:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Every analysis script gets a known-answer test.} Synthetic data
      is acceptable only where the ground truth is \emph{constructed}
      (arithmetic, geometry) and never where it is \emph{rendered}.
\item \textbf{A check that cannot fail on a deliberately broken input is not a
      check.} Several checks in this project passed for weeks and were later
      shown to be unfalsifiable; each now has a fault-injection argument
      (\code{-{}-inject-float-mm}, \code{-{}-self-test}) that makes it fail on
      demand.
\item \textbf{A result contradicting an earlier measurement is an instrument
      check, not a finding}, until the two are reconciled.
\item \textbf{Repeat before believing.} TRAC-IK restarts randomly, so one call
      is one coin flip. Reachability is certified at $N=10$ over a path
      densified to 20\,mm, and usable poses stay 20\,mm inside the last pose
      that passed $10/10$.
\end{itemize}

Chapter~12 gives the catalogue of instrument failure modes this produced,
which is arguably the most reusable output of the project.

\section{What is deliberately absent}

Three whole categories of work were considered and declined, each for a
recorded reason rather than by omission.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No end-to-end vision-language-action policy.} There is no
      corpus; collecting one requires human data that ethics has not cleared;
      the available GPU is an RTX~A500 with 4\,GB of VRAM shared with the
      detector; and a learned policy would learn \emph{around} an unmeasured
      camera extrinsic instead of exposing it.
\item \textbf{No local large language model for command parsing.} A grammar
      was chosen instead, because a grammar can explain a refusal and can be
      swept over 75 phrasings with a scored outcome per phrasing.
\item \textbf{No cyclic (real-time) Kortex control path.} It is unusable over
      WSL2 --- every cyclic write is a network round trip --- and the project
      records an explicit instruction not to spend another day on it.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{The physical platform}

\section{The arms and the mount}

Two Kinova Gen3 seven-degree-of-freedom manipulators, each with a Robotiq
two-finger adaptive gripper and a wrist-mounted colour-and-depth camera module,
are carried on a frame worn on the back. The mount geometry was one of the
longest-running engineering problems in the project and is worth stating as it
was resolved rather than as it was first written.

\subsection{The mount orientation defect}

The arms initially rendered in a posture that did not match the physical rig.
Two hypotheses were possible: the home joint angles were wrong, or the mount
rotation was wrong. The project resolved this by evidence and recorded the
resolution as a hard constraint, because the wrong answer is dangerous:

\keyfact{\textbf{Home joint angles are ground truth and must never be changed
to make a pose look right.} The simulation-to-real bridge replays simulated
joint angles onto the physical arm; any difference it does not catch is
commanded as a jump. The defect was the mount rotation --- specifically an
angle placed in the wrong \code{rpy} slot --- and it was fixed there.}

The mount was then re-derived rather than tuned. A sweep of 108 candidate
mount geometries was recorded and, deliberately, \emph{not} applied; the
finding from it was that no rotation of the mount fixes the binding
constraints, because three of them are not rotational in origin. One
criterion in the original brief was shown to be unsatisfiable by proof rather
than by failure to achieve it.

\subsection{The proximal collision}

A genuine mechanical finding, not a modelling nuisance: with a shoulder-mounted
pair of arms, the segments that threaten the wearer are the proximal ones ---
the upper arm and the tube between shoulder and elbow --- and these are exactly
the pairs a conventional Semantic Robot Description Format (SRDF) file excludes
from collision checking, because on a desk-mounted robot they can never
collide with anything. Forty-four proximal pairs are excluded in this
project's SRDF. The consequence is stated as a hard constraint and repeated
throughout this report:

\keyfact{\code{avoid\_collisions} \textbf{is not the wearer check.} A pose that
MoveIt reports as valid can have the arm's tube inside the person. Wearer
clearance must be measured geometrically. An SRDF exclusion silences an alarm;
it does not move the metal.}

\subsection{The mount caps every clearance figure}

\code{base\_link} sits 0.1610\,m from the wearer's torso and no joint moves it.
The 150\,mm clearance floor therefore has 11\,mm of headroom before the arm has
done anything at all, and a clearance reading of exactly 0.1610 is a
measurement of the mount, not of the arm. This single fact invalidated several
earlier clearance numbers, and the project keeps a dedicated ledger
(\file{docs/system/clearance\_gap\_ledger.md}) recording which historical
workspace figures survived the discovery and which did not.

\section{The master arm}

The master is an instrumented mannequin arm: a potentiometer on each of seven
joints per side, plus a wrist inertial measurement unit, read by a Teensy 4.1
and streamed over serial. It is not a haptic device and provides no force
feedback.

the wiring makes the software freeze channels that now work, and nothing
downstream will disagree with it. Target: twelve or more of fourteen coherent.}

\subsection{Sensing ladder and degraded mode}

Because channels fail, the master sensing path is a ladder rather than a single
algorithm. Position is computed in a spherical mode by default, with a measured
axis map (\code{("+x","+y","+z")}) that is deliberately not ``fixed'' --- the
sign convention of the direction unit vector is documented as correct and
counter-intuitive. A complementary filter fuses the wrist IMU. A gyro-aided
azimuth path exists, is measured, and is not enabled by default. Every channel
is individually disableable through \code{channel\_manager}, and a
\code{degraded\_mode} exists with a measured cost expressed as radial extent
rather than convex hull, because convex hull flatters a degraded arm.

A \code{virtual\_teensy} script provides a synthetic master so that the whole
teleoperation path can be exercised without the physical linkage, and the
serial port is held exclusively so that two readers on one Teensy is
impossible rather than merely detectable --- an earlier incident in which two
\code{master\_pose\_node} instances split the serial stream invalidated a full
day of measurements and is now hard constraint number 3.

\section{The gripper, and a constant that was a curve}

The Robotiq two-finger gripper's fingers swing on a four-bar linkage. The
consequence is that the distance from the wrist frame to the midpoint between
the finger pads is \emph{not} a constant.

This single measurement resolved an argument the repository had with itself for
five days. One earlier finding said the grasp constant was ``13.47\,mm too
long''; another said T1's grasp was ``built 11.43\,mm short''. Both numbers are
points on the same curve, and the two claims were comparing different gripper
states. It also explains why the evidence appeared to flip between runs: a
verification script read the finger tips at whatever opening the simulator
happened to be left at.

The operational consequence is direct. Solving a grasp with the hand open and
then closing it drives the pads 11.43\,mm further along the tool axis --- into
the table. Grasp poses are now solved at the closed-on-object width, and
\code{pad\_mid\_ee\_for(width\_mm)} is the table that supplies the number.

A late and expensive discovery, on 2026-08-25: Fast~DDS shared memory on ROS

domain~7. The real-arm tooling now runs on \code{ROS\_DOMAIN\_ID=7}. In the
same session, an unset \code{RMW\_IMPLEMENTATION} was found to make rclpy
discover nothing at all while the ROS graph was plainly present in other
tools --- a failure that looks exactly like a code bug and is not one.

\section{The follower, and the absence of a planner}

Until 2026-08-22 nobody had written down the following sentence, and it is the
single most clarifying fact about the control path:

\keyfact{\textbf{This project had no motion planner.} \code{ik\_follower\_node}
solves one inverse-kinematics query per commanded pose through MoveIt's
\code{/compute\_ik} (TRAC-IK, \code{solve\_type: Distance}, 5\,ms timeout) and
clamps the joint step per cycle. That is pose streaming with a rate limiter.
There is no OMPL in the command path, no \code{move\_group.plan()}, no
\code{compute\_cartesian\_path}. MoveIt is used for exactly two things: the IK
service and the planning scene.}

For teleoperation that is the correct architecture. The operator's hand is the
input, there is no goal to plan to, and the real problem is smooth, safe
following. For autonomy it is not correct, and the cost was measured: a pick
whose pregrasp and grasp poses are both individually clear, streamed as a
straight line in joint space, breaches the wearer clearance floor at 0.1462\,m
at step 29 of 64. The path checker catches it --- and catching it means the run
stops. Chapter~8 describes the joint-space planner built in response.

\item Arms down the sides: the left column \emph{improves} by 25\,mm

consequence: folding the arms is worth 50\,mm to the mannequin and nothing at
all to this person.}

\section{Neither arm crosses the centreline}

Measured at every cross-side placement slot and every cross-side object: 0 of
10 IK solutions, both arms. This is not marginal --- nothing solves. It is a
hard architectural fact that decides what the two-arm task can be: an object
can only be delivered to the target on its own side, so the randomised stage of
the pick-and-place task draws the \emph{side} and lets the colour follow, and
the task builder refuses a cube whose colour names the other side's pad.

The same fact killed a planned inter-arm handover task outright: the two arms'
reachable sets are disjoint, so there is no point at which one can hand to the
other. That is recorded as a finding, not as a failure to implement.

\section{The home pose}

\subsection{The change, and the two things it was}

On 2026-08-15 the simulated home pose became the ``presentation pose'': wrists
level, hands in front of the chest. The change was made only after the entire
task set was re-measured, and it was carefully separated into two changes of
which only one is dangerous.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Moving home} cost nothing measurable. With the workspace anchor
      unchanged, every task performs exactly as before.
\item \textbf{Re-deriving the anchor to match a level home} costs task T2 its
      right arm, so it is refused by name in the hard constraints.
\end{itemize}

\keyfact{\textbf{Simulated and real home disagree on purpose.} The physical
arms are still at the legacy Kortex home, where the wrists point up by
$+85$\dg{} and $+79$\dg. The bridge \emph{refuses to enable} on the roughly
1.94\,rad difference and says why, rather than commanding it as a jump. The new
pose must be captured on the arms before any real session.}

\subsection{The pose in pixels}

A separate investigation asked whether the rendered home pose was wrong, and
found that the render was right and the numbers were about something else.
Measured from the transform tree at boot: elbows 0.0793\,m (left) and 0.2715\,m
(right) below their shoulders; the left elbow 188\,mm outboard of its own hand;
hands 1.10\,m apart against a 0.36\,m torso; forearm mirror residual 0.2826\,m.
The wrists are level to $-0.01$\dg{} and always were. It is not a cached
configuration: the live joint state matches the source file to $7\times10^{-5}$\,rad.

Can it be fixed? Partly. The innermost hand column that works for both arms is
$|x| = 0.450$ against a torso half-width of 0.18 --- so ``hands at torso width''
is, once again, the torso. The best achievable mirror residual is
0.161--0.164\,m and does not improve with more search branches (4 branches
versus 31 changes it by 1\,mm), so the asymmetry is structural: the two mounts'
base axes mirror exactly but differ by 168\dg{} of roll, and two identical arms
on mirrored mounts cannot mirror. This was measured and deliberately not
applied.

\subsection{A run must start from home}

From the second run of a session onwards, it did not. Nothing returned the arms
to home when a run ended and nothing checked at the start, so run 2 began
1.2338\,rad (left) and 0.6248\,rad (right) away from home, measured at the first
commanded waypoint. Boot and run 1 both read 0.0000. The task runner now stages
before the task and \emph{refuses} rather than running from an unknown pose;
\code{-{}-allow-unhomed} overrides, loudly. The recorded clip sweep always
staged, so the recorded clips were never affected.

\chapter{Teleoperation}

\section{The command path}

Master pose is read, mapped through the spherical position mode and the
measured axis map, scaled, gated by a clutch, and turned into a commanded
end-effector pose. The IK follower solves one query per pose and hands joint
targets to the motion generator. Orientation is held at the workspace anchor by
default; mode 03 and 04 replace it with an autonomy-supplied wrist.

The clutch is a genuine engineering component rather than a button: a boot
reference that was originally the master frame origin (wrong, and fixed), a
live button map with a lab check, a second press that cancels a pending
engagement, and an indexing behaviour measured over ten cycles against the
virtual board. The first such run showed four cycles of 0.00\,mm, which was a
real bug and not a measurement artefact --- a distinction the project makes
explicitly because the reverse is far more common here.

\section{Motion generation: from a clamp to Ruckig}

Until 2026-08-23 the follower's motion generation was a per-joint step clamp.
Each joint was clamped independently, which has three consequences that were
measured rather than argued:

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}

\midrule

This is not a tuning result. Swept against its own step size, the clamp's
excursion \emph{settles} at 68\,mm --- there is no step size that fixes it,
because the defect is the independence of the per-joint clamps, not their
magnitude.

Ruckig was selected on published characteristics (time-optimal, jerk-limited,
type V, 19.8\,\textmu s mean for seven degrees of freedom, no dependencies)
and adopted as the default in every mode. Velocity limits are the robot's own;
acceleration and jerk are \textbf{assumed and labelled as such}, because
\file{joint\_limits.yaml} declares \code{has\_acceleration\_limits: false} for
all fourteen joints. \code{motion\_generator:=legacy} reproduces any recording
made before 2026-08-23.

Five defects in the author's own implementation were caught by checks during
this work, and all five are instructive: re-planning every cycle destroyed
phase synchronisation; what forced the re-plan was floating-point noise; the
fallback path claimed to be jerk-limited by reporting zeros; the off-path
metric had a 0.05\,mm floor and reported that floor as behaviour; and the
arrival-cycle test used an absolute tolerance on a per-joint quantity.

\section{The simulation-to-real gap, measured}

\keyfact{\textbf{Every joint parks 0.305\dg{} short of its target, on the side
it came from.} 211 of 252 recorded per-joint errors fall at $\pm$0.25--0.30\dg{}
with the sign a coin flip --- which makes it a \emph{bound}, not a spread.}

At the end effector that is 7.2\,mm RMS on a 100\,mm move, on both arms.
Pushing the recorded joint errors through the Jacobian reproduces the Cartesian
error to 90\,\% ($r = 0.923$), which establishes that it is a constant terminal
offset rather than a gain --- two models that are indistinguishable at 100\,mm
and differ by a factor of five at 20\,mm. A single parameter per arm removes
74\,\% and 76\,\% of it, held out on an axis the parameter was never fitted on,
where a full Cartesian $3\times3$ correction scores 93.5\,mm. The compensation
is ``overshoot each joint by $\varepsilon$ in the direction of travel'': no
matrix, no basis, no step length.

\keyfact{The data for this had existed in
\file{recordings/baselines/arm\_directional\_calibration.json} since it was
recorded, and \textbf{nothing read it}.}

A correction of the project's own earlier reporting belongs here. A ``speed
sweep'' was reported as showing the error to be speed-independent across a
$5\times$ range. It was not a sweep: \code{vmax\_rad\_s} was read once at bridge
start-up, and 7 of 36 runs moved faster than the limit they were nominally
commanded with. The three speeds are three replicates of one condition, and how
the error varies with speed has never been measured. A control test now asserts
that the sweep still looks inert, so that a genuine sweep will make it fail and
force the stale sentences to be rewritten.

\section{The bridge deadband}

The high-level bridge had a 1.0\dg{} deadband inside which the proportional
term is off by design --- $3.3\times$ the 0.305\dg{} park error it was
permitting. It is now 0.10\dg, with an opt-in \code{terminal\_overshoot} that
applies the compensation at the setpoint and refuses to run in the ``on'' state
with a zero overshoot value. Neither has been tried on hardware; both are live
parameters and four runs would settle which wins.

\chapter{The virtual-reality path, which is really motion capture}

\section{The route decision}

The second input route uses a Meta Quest headset and its two controllers. The
route was decided on facts rather than preference, because the obvious route
was blocked: there is no \code{adb} on this machine, so a sideloaded native
application was never available.

What was built instead is a WebXR client served by
\code{quest\_bridge\_node} directly to the headset's own browser over the
laboratory Wi-Fi. The node serves \emph{both} the client page and the secure
WebSocket from one TLS origin, which means one certificate exception covers
both. The certificate carries the machine's IP address in
\code{subjectAltName}, and WSL2's mirrored networking makes this box's LAN
address identical to Windows'.

\keyfact{The advantage of being forced onto the browser route is that it works
on a \emph{borrowed} headset: no sideloading, no developer account, no settings
changed.}

Running this path for the first time on 2026-08-19 found two defects that no
amount of reading would have found:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The bridge node had never started at all.} Not once. A member
      assignment \code{self.clients} shadowed a read-only \code{Node} property,
      and the node raised on construction.
\item \textbf{Its axis map was for the standard ROS frame, not this
      repository's.} A hand held three metres forward commanded three metres to
      the wearer's \emph{right}.
\end{itemize}

\section{It is not virtual reality; it is motion capture}

The shipped operating arrangement, clarified on 2026-08-19, is:

\keyfact{The operator sits \textbf{across the room, facing the wearer}, holds
the controllers as six-degree-of-freedom motion capture, and watches the robot
directly. \textbf{Nobody wears the headset} --- it stands on a shelf as the
tracking reference.}

This has a geometric consequence that had been promised in a docstring for
months and never implemented. Facing someone and copying their motion is a
\emph{reflection} --- determinant $-1$ --- which mirrors every orientation while
positions still look approximately right. The alignment is therefore a
\emph{yaw}, never a mirror, and no value of the parameter can produce one. The
yaw is measured rather than guessed by
\file{scripts/calibrate\_operator\_yaw.py}, which refuses a calibration motion
under 100\,mm or more than 35\dg{} off horizontal.

One ambiguity this uncovered and did not settle: the repository disagrees with
itself about whether world $+x$ is the wearer's right, and the arms are
physically mounted the other way round. That is recorded as open.

\section{Three safety gaps that desk operation turns from rare into routine}

\subsection{The tracking reference can be knocked}

In desk operation the headset \emph{is} the tracking reference, and a nudge
announces nothing: poses stay valid, the rate stays 90\,Hz, controllers stay
tracked, and every pose after the knock is expressed in a rotated frame.
\code{vr\_safety\_node} now latches the first head-mounted-display pose and
freezes past 20\,mm or 2\dg. The freeze deliberately survives the ordinary
unfreeze path, because poses flow the entire time the frame is wrong.
\code{/vr/rebase\_reference} is a deliberate act and tells the operator to
re-calibrate.

\subsection{Controller tracking loss froze nothing}

\code{/vr/tracking\_ok} was derived from the arrival time of the last frame,
stamped \emph{before} the per-controller validity check. It therefore caught a
dropout, a sleeping headset and a backgrounded application --- but not the case
it is named after. Measured with the real Quest: covering a controller gave
\textbf{zero} loss transitions while the stream ran on at 90\,Hz. There is now
a per-hand \code{/vr/controller\_valid\_<hand>}, and the mapper freezes that arm
and refuses to engage it.

\subsection{Nothing consumed the freeze}

The safety node computed freezes that no node subscribed to. This is the
project's canonical ``feature present but does nothing'' defect: a value was
being \emph{stored} correctly and no consumer \emph{read} it.

\section{The observer e-stop}

An interlock required \code{/vr/observer\_estop\_present} before real motion.
Nothing ever published it, which made the interlock a permanent refusal ---
exactly the kind of gate somebody routes around. It is now a heartbeat:
\file{scripts/observer\_estop.py} publishes at 5\,Hz, re-confirms every 30\,s,
and \code{vr\_safety\_node} treats two seconds of silence as \emph{withdrawal}.
It had previously been a latch, so a publisher that stopped left the system
believing somebody was still standing there holding the button.

\section{The mapper}

\code{vr\_pose\_mapper} holds the arms while it runs, so the staging move
cannot execute concurrently; the recording sweep stops it for staging and
re-isolates it afterwards. Its more damaging defect --- it did not reset
between runs, which cost mode 02 every grasping task --- is fixed in the node
itself: \code{reset()}, a \code{/vr/reset} trigger service and a
\code{/vr/reset\_request} topic clear references, anchors, the filter and the
thumbstick scale, and the mode isolation calls it on \emph{every} run and
refuses the mode if it will not answer. The accumulating term was the rate
limiter, which is now published as \code{lag\_m} so it can be seen.

A related visibility defect: the mapper published its state only inside the
engaged branch, which made ``disengaged'', ``reset'', ``frozen'' and ``the
process died'' one single observation. A 2\,Hz idle heartbeat now carries the
same keys with \code{engaged: false}.

\keyfact{\textbf{Mode 02 still cannot grasp.} All three of its grasping tasks
fail; the pads miss by 88\,mm rising to 206\,mm, against a 30\,mm gate, and the
miss \emph{accumulates}. The other four modes close at 0.0000\,m.}

\section{One button starts VR}

\code{START VR TELEOP} at the top of the GUI's RUN tab runs twelve steps in an
order that is load-bearing --- the simulation before the mapper, the certificate
before the bridge, the port before the bridge --- each with a plain-words
failure message and the fix presented as a button. It is idempotent: pressing
it on a running system says ``already done''. It cannot reach a real arm, by
construction and by test. \file{scripts/verify\_vr\_one\_button.py} runs it for
real with nothing typed: 22 checks, 0 failed, 10 of 10 machine-side steps,
stopping honestly at the absent headset.

Three findings came out of building it, and all three are about self-inflicted
faults:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{``READY TO OPERATE'' with no headset in the building.} The final
      step checked that the pose topic was \emph{listed}, and the bridge
      creates its publishers at start-up --- so the topic exists the moment the
      bridge does. The operator would have squeezed the grip, watched nothing
      happen, and had a window still saying ready. It counts message
      \emph{arrivals} over a window now.
\item \textbf{Clearing stale shared memory wedges the ROS daemon}, and the
      bring-up then blamed the simulation --- false, specific and confident,
      which is the worst combination, because it sends you to the wrong log.
      Measured: after a clear, \code{ros2 topic list} returned 2 topics against
      a fully running stack.
\item \textbf{Three repairs recreated their own fault.} Clearing shared memory
      restarts the daemon, which orphans the daemon's own segments --- four
      repairs in a row. And the sweep destroyed a \emph{starting} simulation,
      because a process coming up has segments it has not mapped yet, so they
      look unowned. Staleness is now asked of the kernel through
      \file{/proc/*/maps} with a 20\,s age floor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Passthrough}

Passthrough (\code{immersive-ar} in the Quest's own browser) is built and is an
\emph{addition}. It adds nothing to the arm's latency, and that claim is
structural rather than measured: there is one \code{requestSession} call site
and one frame loop serving both modes, pinned by a test. It reports
\code{environmentBlendMode}, because a granted AR session that composites
\code{opaque} shows the operator a black room while the page believes it showed
them the laboratory. In desk operation nobody wears the headset, so passthrough
is worth nothing there --- which is stated rather than quietly omitted.

\chapter{Perception}

\section{Detection is not the bottleneck}

This is the most important sentence in the perception chapter and it is
deliberately placed first.

\keyfact{On 2026-08-21 both real arms were driven repeatedly and the perception

colour-only filter three times. \textbf{Nothing was picked up.} The reasons
were calibration, kinematics, and eight defects in the code that commands the
arms. A better detector fixes nothing that is currently broken.}

Two design decisions are recorded with their reasons. The grasp is geometric
and deliberately not learned: a parallel jaw on a rigid object is a solved

could not explain a refusal. And range is used as a discriminator where colour
cannot be: the teal robots in the laboratory have a shadowed hue of 76 and the
target green cube has 74 --- two apart, inside noise --- but the robots stand
2.8\,m away while the rig works under 1.6\,m, so range separates them cleanly.

\section{Measure the environment, then plan against it}

The operator's own words prompted this work: \emph{``there is so much space to
calibrate, why can't you create an algorithm to properly figure out the
environment''}. The criticism was exactly right.

\keyfact{Almost everything in this repository that touched an object was
\textbf{told where it is}. The pick-and-place task built its path from four
coordinates in a source file; the table was at 1.250\,m because a constant said
so. All of it was verified at $N=10$ over densified paths, and \textbf{none of
it survives a different table, a different object, or the same object moved
50\,mm}. The number in the file becomes a lie that the arm acts on
confidently.}

Three pieces that could have sensed instead already existed and had never been
composed: the table analyser was used only to \emph{print a description}; the
planner's \code{VoxelWorld} had been constructed nowhere outside its own
self-test, so no path this project ever planned knew that the table was there;
and \code{work\_surface.set\_measured()} existed precisely so the surface height
could come from depth, and nothing called it.

\subsection{The sweep: a printer probe, not a pan-and-scan}

Two further pieces of operator feedback drove the design: \emph{``the gripper
and the whole joints move everywhere without the gripper being stable''} and
\emph{``imagine how a 3D printer works\ldots our robot is in 3 space, it should
map all the sides with all the lengths''}.

The first was the important one. The wrist had been \emph{re-aimed at every
cell}: the tool axis pointed at each grid point, so the orientation changed
between neighbours 90\,mm apart. The arm swung continuously and no two views
were taken from the same attitude --- which means a residual wrist error was a
\emph{different} error in every frame and could never cancel or be measured.

A printer's probe does not re-aim. It holds one attitude and translates. So
does this sweep now: one wrist direction per pass, with the camera grid being
the work grid rigidly translated back along that fixed ray, so consecutive
cells differ by a pure translation. Several passes at different yaws supply the
sides, because a side is invisible from a single elevation. The sweep is
2 layers $\times$ 3 yaws $\times$ 24 cells $\times$ 2 arms $=$ 144 cells and
13.4\,m of travel, in a straight serpentine (1.140\,m of travel against 1.702\,m
for the obvious alternative --- measured, not asserted).

\keyfact{\textbf{Stationary is not the same as arrived.} A joint can sit
0.02\,rad from its commanded value and still be moving through it, and a frame
captured at that moment is smeared across two poses while every downstream
statistic reports fine. \code{is\_still} watches for 400\,ms and requires no
joint to move more than 0.0015\,rad --- a tenth of the arrival tolerance.}

\subsection{Two lifetimes, one command}

Bundling reachability and scene mapping into one command hid that they are
facts about different things:

\begin{itemize}
\item \file{reachable\_cells.json} is a fact about the \textbf{robot}: which
      camera poses the arm can reach. It is a one-time measurement, keyed on
      every bound, layer, facing, elevation and step, so moving the mount,
      changing home or editing the URDF invalidates it automatically.
\item \file{world\_map.json} is a fact about the \textbf{scene}. Somebody moves
      a cube and it is wrong immediately.
\end{itemize}

There is no incremental re-map: \code{build()} fuses every view from scratch,
which is fine for ``new cell, new table'' and much too slow for ``I put a
different object down''. Until that exists, \code{pick\_from\_map} reports the
map's age on every run and \textbf{refuses to move off a map older than 30
minutes}. Planning from an old map is harmless and only reported; driving the
arm at coordinates measured in a previous session is the failure that costs
something, and a stale file looks exactly as authoritative as a fresh one.

\chapter{Autonomy, language, and planning}

\section{Why a grammar and not a language model}

grammar can explain a refusal, can be swept exhaustively over a phrase set with
a scored outcome per phrase, and fits inside a 4\,GB VRAM budget already
occupied by the detector. The stronger version of the same argument is recorded
against end-to-end vision-language-action policies, with an instruction not to
reverse it later without reading the reasoning.

\section{The window is part of the parser}

The GUI's \code{Instruct} tab is not a text box that shells out. It shows, in

\section{Voice}

Voice reaches the same parser over \code{/voice\_transcript}. The honesty
constraint here is unusually strict and is repeated in three documents:

\keyfact{\file{/dev/snd} on this machine contains only \code{timer}, so no
microphone can be opened. Every voice number in this repository was produced
from Piper-\emph{synthesised} audio --- word error rate 25.2\,\%, zero exact
transcripts, one voice, no room, no noise. \textbf{A voice score in this
repository is a parser score, never a transcription score.} Speech recognition
accuracy against a real speaker is unmeasured.}

\section{The mislabel control}

An autonomy system that plans from a declared object property rather than an
observed one will do the wrong thing confidently, so a control exists to prove
which one is in force. A cube is declared green and rendered blue. The declared
path sends it to the green pad at $x = -0.290$ on the \emph{right} arm; the
camera's path sends it to the blue pad at $x = +0.290$ on the \emph{left} ---
0.580\,m apart and a different arm entirely.

That control had been refusing to run since the task rebuild, because its own
single-arm look cannot see a row that straddles the centreline. It now takes
\code{-{}-vision}: the staged two-arm detections that the recorded clip was
itself planned from.

\section{The planner that was built}

\file{joint\_planner.py} is RRT-Connect in joint space, with every design choice
made for this specific robot:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Joint space, not Cartesian.} The binding constraint is the
      distance from the whole \emph{arm} to the wearer, not from the hand. A
      Cartesian planner would solve IK at every node anyway.
\item \textbf{The wearer floor is the collision check}, through the same
      moving-chain clearance the guard enforces --- not the SRDF, which is
      blind to the pairs that matter.
\item \textbf{Every edge densified at 0.05\,rad}, matching the path checker. An
      RRT that checks only its nodes reproduces the streaming path's defect one
      level up.
\item \textbf{Straight line tried first.} Most moves are fine, and planning a
      clear move turns 60\,ms into seconds.
\item \textbf{Shortcut, then resample to the follower's step.} Handing the
      follower waypoints further apart than its maximum step means it invents
      the intermediate motion with an unchecked straight interpolation --- the
      exact defect the planner exists to remove, reintroduced at the last step.
\end{itemize}

The alternatives were assessed against the same 4\,GB ceiling and recorded with
sources: cuRobo is the strongest thing in the space and needs roughly 2.5\,GB at
batch 1024 on a 48\,GB card, so it is the right answer on a bigger machine;
Isaac Lab's minimum specification is 8\,GB and current documentation lists
16\,GB; MoveIt Servo is the right \emph{shape} for teleoperation and is worth
evaluating; MoveIt's own octomap is the right idea with an implementation known
for voxels that never clear, which is why this project builds a per-frame
\code{VoxelWorld} and deliberately does not carry occupancy between frames.

\code{pick\_from\_map} is the \code{VoxelWorld}'s first consumer outside its own
self-test: pregrasp, grasp, lift, carry, place, retreat, with every coordinate
taken from the measured map and fifteen narration phases published on
\code{/robot\_say} and shown in the window.

\section{Fault injection}

The project's confidence in its safety layer comes from injection rather than
from reading. The injection suite went 8/10, then 10/10, then \textbf{14/14},
and six full experiments were run with faults injected mid-trial and all six
passed.

\keyfact{Four harness bugs were found during that work, \textbf{all of which
made a failed injection look like a pass}. That is the transferable lesson: a
fault-injection harness is itself an instrument, and it must be cleared before
its verdicts are believed.}

Two structural fixes came out of it:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The dead-man's real hole was the source timestamp.} It could not
      see the failure that actually happens --- a publisher that keeps
      publishing stale content with fresh stamps.
\item \textbf{An expired blocker is not permission to move.} Automatic expiry
      of a blocking condition had been treated as clearance; it is now an
      explicit state that must be resolved.
\end{itemize}

\section{The latch pattern}

Several components had a latch that could not be reset by the disappearance of
the thing it was latching --- the observer e-stop believed somebody was present
after the publisher stopped; the follower's e-stop block was invisible exactly
when it mattered. The systemic fix was to convert presence latches into
heartbeats with an explicit silence timeout, and this pattern now appears in
three separate nodes.

\section{Anonymity, enforced in code}

\code{write\_manifest()} raises on \code{name}, \code{email}, \code{dob},
\code{address} and \code{phone}. This is a code-level constraint rather than a
policy document, because a policy document does not stop a keystroke.

\section{And a demonstration is not evidence}

Demonstration clips carry that caveat in their caption and produce no trial
data, no condition and no metric. This is hard constraint 13, and it exists to
stop a persuasive video from being cited as a result.

\chapter{The window}

\section{The rule}

On 2026-08-22, at the operator's request, the project adopted a rule that
changed how every subsequent feature was delivered:

\keyfact{\textbf{The window is the interface. Every capability reaches it, and
every change is checked in it.} A feature reachable only from a terminal does
not exist for the person running the session.}

Four clauses follow from it. If you add or change a capability, it gets a
control in the GUI \emph{in the same change}. A press must leave a trace, and a
control that changes what the next run does must say so --- which is why every
toggle, drop-down and spin box logs its new value. After pressing everything,
the window must still work: the audit re-checks the layout, the tabs, the
e-stop and the instruction box at the \emph{end} of its sweep, because a
control that works on a fresh window and stops working after somebody explored
the panel is a real defect and the audit is the only thing positioned to catch
it. And you must run the audit before claiming anything works.

\section{What the window is}

One command --- \code{bash scripts/start\_gui.sh} --- sources everything,
refuses a second window, and opens the GUI. RViz, showing the \emph{commanded}
state, is embedded where a window manager exists (detected from the X root
window, not assumed), beside a native panel drawn from the \code{real\_*}
frames showing the \emph{actual} state, with a divergence readout under both.
The left column is grouped SET UP / RUN / STATUS. A connection panel names
eight faults in plain words and offers the fix as a button.

\section{Worked examples}

Four are worth stating in full, because the mechanism in each is subtle.

\subsection{A render check that could not fail}

Removing a bounding box left a label reading its corners, so the render tick
raised an exception on frame 1 and the scene came up with no table, no cubes,
no pads and no workspace marking. The check filed it as OK at 18\,\% ink,
\emph{because the wearer is 18\,\% of the frame} --- and a real scene reads
46--50\,\%. The renderer now subscribes to \code{/task\_objects} and refuses to
shoot without the task's own namespaces present.

\subsection{The test gate could invent a failure}

\code{check\_tests.py} matched \code{\^{}FAILED (\textbackslash S+)} over the
whole pytest output --- and the docstring checker \emph{echoes} the source lines
it objects to. So the string \code{FAILED = "failed"} appearing in a source file
became a failing test named \code{=} on a suite where everything passed.

\keyfact{A gate that cries wolf is a gate that somebody bypasses. It is now
pinned by \file{test\_the\_test\_gate\_cannot\_invent\_a\_failure.py}.}

\subsection{The frame extractor never extracted a grasp}

It mapped event wall-clocks through the \emph{scene node's} start time. Under
mode 06 that is about 300\,s before the video capture begins, so every grasp
event landed at $+380$\,s inside a 48\,s clip and was skipped in silence. It
printed ``10 frames'' and exited 0 --- openings and final frames only. The
sweep now writes a capture clock into \file{clip\_meta.json}, and the extractor
refuses a clip it cannot map and fails when no grasp frame comes out.

\subsection{Six verifiers asking the wrong question}

The single most expensive instrument failure in the project's history:

\begin{itemize}
\item \file{verify\_msc\_tasks.py} had raised \code{AttributeError} \emph{on
      import} since T1 became two-armed --- it had not run at all.
\item About twenty scripts solved IK at the live \emph{home} wrist, which is
      42.9\dg{} from the anchor the task runner actually commands. One task's
      own pick reads 10/10 at the anchor and 0/10 at home.
\item They asked for an \emph{exact} orientation while the follower has run a
      15\dg{} cone since 2026-08-22. Correcting that took T1 stage 1 from 32 IK
      failures to 0, and the choreography routine's total from 171 to 68.
\item \code{normalise()} returned radians while its two sibling functions took
      degrees.
\item \code{verify\_t1 -{}-stage2 N} overwrote the stage 1 baseline file.
\end{itemize}

\section{The cube pick, 2026-08-25}

The first sustained attempt to drive a real arm from its own gripper camera.
The grasp geometry was never the hard part; every hour went to instruments that
reported confident wrong answers.

\subsection{The finding that governs everything else}

\keyfact{\textbf{The gripper camera's extrinsic calibration is wrong by about
8.5\dg, and it rotates with the wrist.} The table cannot move, so the table
normal measured from any arm pose must be the same vector. Measured from four
poses: 9.74\dg, 15.42\dg, 16.26\dg{} and 18.14\dg{} off world vertical --- a
worst pairwise disagreement of \textbf{8.45\dg}, which is 34\,mm of position
error at 0.23\,m and 59\,mm at 0.40\,m. No open-loop grasp planned through that
transform can land inside the 30\,mm capture gate, and none did.}

The depth camera itself is excellent and was never the problem: the table plane
fits at 94.8\,\% inliers with 0.46\,mm RMS, and a real 40\,mm cube measures
$39.2 \times 38.9 \times 40.9$\,mm. Only the transform out of the camera is
bad. The pick therefore servos closed-loop in the camera's own frame, where the
data is good, and nulls the pad-to-cube error by iteration --- because a closed
loop converges through a wrong extrinsic and an open loop cannot.

\subsection{Defects in the vendored camera stack}

\begin{itemize}
\item The vendored \code{kinova\_vision} launch file publishes a static
      transform putting \code{camera\_link} to \code{depth} at
      $(-0.0195, -0.005, 0)$ against the robot description's
      $(-0.0275, -0.0096, 0)$ --- 8.0\,mm and 4.6\,mm apart, and two writers on
      one static edge.
\item Both arms publish the \emph{same unprefixed} \code{camera\_link}, so left
      and right collide in one global transform tree. A \code{launch\_tf}
      argument (default true, behaviour unchanged) now allows the frames to be
      remapped onto the description's.
\item \code{loadCameraInfo()} declares its parameters unconditionally and runs
      again on reconnect, so all four camera nodes abort with
      \code{ParameterAlreadyDeclaredException} the moment a stream recovers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the pick pipeline now does}

Two commands: \code{bash scripts/bringup\_arm.sh left} (one Kortex session, the
camera on the description's frames, idempotent), then
\code{python3 scripts/srl\_pick\_cube.py}, which finds a viewpoint that actually
\emph{sees} the cube, measures the table plane and the cube in the camera's own
frame, servos the pads onto the cube, closes, lifts and verifies.

Three geometric facts are built into it. The wrist camera cannot see its own
grasp --- the last 80\,mm push the cube out of the bottom of the frame, which is
geometry and not failure, so the last good fix is carried in world coordinates
and the remainder completed from it. All joint arithmetic goes through
\code{ang\_wrap}. And motion is judged only through a freshness-checked joint
read.

\keyfact{\textbf{The outstanding safety gap, stated plainly:} the wearer
clearance model samples link \emph{origins} only, so it misses the arm's body
between joints. Until it measures link \emph{geometry}, a ``pass'' from it means
nothing --- and hard constraint 11 is therefore currently unenforced in code.}

\keyfact{The floor is a \emph{distance}, not a reaction time, and nothing in
this system reacts to a wearer who moves quickly. That is the single most
important control problem in the project, and it is not a software bug --- it is
the consequence of a 150\,mm margin, a roughly half-second perception chain, and
a person who can move a metre in that time.}

Three things would help, in order of honesty, and none is done:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Inflate the floor by the reaction budget.} The guard should keep
      \emph{floor} $+$ \emph{latency} $\times$ \emph{assumed speed}, not a fixed
      150\,mm. That is a one-parameter change and it makes the margin mean
      something.
\item \textbf{Cut the latency.} 562\,ms is the rate limit, not the tracker ---
      the tracker itself is 28--34\,ms per frame. Most of the spread is
      recoverable.
\item \textbf{Slow the arm near the person.} Speed is already a live parameter
      and clearance is already computed. Nothing currently couples them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The failure mode of losing tracking}

\code{fuse()} is correct about its own rule --- the camera may only make the
wearer bigger --- and it falls back to the mannequin when the estimate is stale,
silent or gated out. \textbf{That fallback is the gap.} If the wearer has raised
their arms and tracking drops, the guard reverts to the assumed \code{down}
posture and believes there is 317\,mm of clearance where there is 93\,mm.

The system's current answer is an instruction to the wearer, recorded as hard
constraint 11. That is a real mitigation and it is not a control system.

\keyfact{Markerless tracking here is not a nicety; it is load bearing --- and
its failure mode is to quietly restore an assumption that is 224\,mm
optimistic.}

\section{Blocked on ethics}

No human data has been collected at all. Operator and wearer are different
people and consent separately. Worn operation means more than 17\,kg on
somebody's back with no gravity compensation, moving under partly autonomous
control, within reach of a head and torso, driven by a person who did not
choose the wearer's motion. The ethics document describes the study as it is
actually built, \emph{including the parts that are not ready}, on the stated
principle that an ethics application which overstates readiness is the wrong
kind of document.

\section{Blocked on measurement}

Ranked by how much they cost if left alone:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The gripper camera's extrinsic calibration.} Sized at 8.45\dg{}
      of wrist-dependent rotation; it is the largest term in the positioning
      budget and it is the reason no open-loop grasp has landed.
\item \textbf{The wearer clearance model.} It samples link origins, not link
      geometry, and returns the same number for an arm inside a person and an
      arm in free space. Hard constraint 11 is not enforced in code.
\item \textbf{The reaction budget.} The floor does not include the latency
      term.
\item \textbf{How the terminal joint error varies with speed.} Never measured;
      the run that was reported as a speed sweep was three replicates of one
      condition.
\item \textbf{The size of the between-modes difference.} The effect is real and
      its magnitude is unknown, because the recorded set cannot separate mode
      from the node set each mode brings.
\item \textbf{T1's exact grasp}, which is refused by T1's own table: it solves
      10/10 with no furniture and needs a 5.0\dg{} tilt with the table present.
      The follower's cone supplies the tilt, at a cost of 9.58\,mm measured
      against 8.58\,mm predicted --- inside the gate, and not zero.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Built but not yet fed, or not yet wired}

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{/viz/envelope} and \code{/viz/refused\_in} are built and tested and
      not yet published to.
\item Predictive avoidance has a real null-space implementation --- Jacobian by
      finite differences, closed-loop task-space correction with an
      end-effector residual of 0.00001\,m --- and is worth 0.0 to 5.6\,mm.
      Every one of 28 refusals names the end-effector link, because the
      \emph{hand} is what is inside the person and the null space holds the
      hand fixed by definition. It is not wired into the follower, and now for a
      better reason than before.
\item An incremental re-map. The world model rebuilds from scratch, which makes
      ``I moved one object'' a 13-minute operation.
\item The LeRobot export exists and is worth having; the policies are blocked
      three independent ways (no corpus, 4\,GB VRAM, and a policy would learn

\section{What was built}

A complete shared-autonomy stack for a two-armed wearable robot: three input
routes (a physical master linkage, six-degree-of-freedom motion capture, and
natural language), five operating modes, a perception pipeline that measures
its environment rather than being told about it, a jerk-limited motion
generator, a joint-space planner whose collision check is the wearer rather
than the SRDF, a safety architecture built on heartbeats rather than latches, a
single operating window through which every capability is reachable, and a
verification apparatus of 114 test modules, 233 analysis scripts and 195
recorded baselines.

\section{What was learned}

The engineering is replaceable; the method is not. Three lessons generalise
beyond this robot.

\keyfact{\textbf{1. A surprising measurement is evidence about the instrument.}
Seventeen times in six weeks, the surprising result was the instrument and not
the system. The cost of checking the instrument first is minutes; the cost of
not doing so was, on several occasions, a day.}

\keyfact{\textbf{2. A check that cannot fail is worse than no check}, because
it converts an unknown into a false belief. Several of this project's checks
passed for weeks while measuring nothing: a clearance floor compared against a
constant that included the immobile mount; a tray that stretched to meet
whichever hands existed; a render check satisfied by the wearer's own pixels.
Every check here now has an argument that makes it fail on demand.}

\keyfact{\textbf{3. Say what is unmeasured.} The single most useful line in the
control budget is the row that reads UNMEASURED, because it is the row that
tells the next session what to go and do. A budget with a guessed term in it
would have looked better and would have hidden the 8.45\dg{} extrinsic error
that was costing every grasp.}

\section{Where it stands}

The system sees well, refuses well, and explains itself well. It does not yet
place a hand accurately enough to close a grasp from an open-loop plan, and it
cannot react to a person who moves quickly. Both of those are named, sized, and
have a next action. Nothing has been worn, and no human data exists; that is a
deliberate ordering, not an oversight.

\chapter{The hard constraints, in full}

These are the project's standing rules. Each one exists because violating it
caused, or would cause, real damage. They are reproduced here because they are
the most compressed statement of what the system is.

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{-1}
\item \textbf{Simulated and real home disagree on purpose.} Simulated home is
      the presentation pose; the physical arms are still at the legacy Kortex
      home, where the wrists point up by $+85$\dg{} and $+79$\dg. The bridge
      refuses to enable on the roughly 1.9\,rad difference rather than
      commanding it.
\item \textbf{Home joint angles are ground truth, and the real arm's are the
      ones that count.} Never change either to make a pose look right without
      re-measuring first. The workspace anchor is a \emph{different} quantity:
      moving home cost nothing measurable, while re-deriving the anchor to
      match a level home costs task T2 its right arm.
\item \textbf{The arm permits exactly one Kortex session.} SIGINT the bridge;
      SIGKILL leaks the session and the next run cannot connect.
\item \textbf{Never run two stacks.} Two master pose nodes split the serial
      stream and invalidated a full day of measurements. A stray
      \code{robot\_state\_publisher} does the same damage and is not caught by
      a process count.
\item \textbf{The Kortex cyclic path is unusable over WSL.} Use the high-level
      API. Every cyclic write is a network round trip.
\item \textbf{\code{FASTDDS\_BUILTIN\_TRANSPORTS=SHM}.} UDP discovery is dead
      on this host. Clear \file{/dev/shm/fastrtps\_*} with the stack stopped,
      then relaunch.
\item \textbf{Never wait for a service in the e-stop trip path.} It once
      blocked the e-stop for 4.0\,s. Park first, then talk to the driver.
\item \textbf{The dependency arrow runs one way.} \code{srl\_teleop} imports
      nothing in-repository; it is the baseline condition of every experiment.
\item \textbf{Real-robot mode must be armed.} Motion is disabled until enabled
      by hand, and it refuses to start with a continuous joint wound beyond
      $\pm\pi$.
\item \textbf{Never \code{pkill} broad patterns while a stack you want is
      running.} Kill an explicit PID list, and kill the process group.
\item \textbf{Wrap ROS \file{setup.bash} in \code{set +u} / \code{set -u}} ---
      it reads unbound variables and dies otherwise.
\item \textbf{The wearer is in the collision model and the clearance floor is
      the last thing between the arms and a person's chest.} Do not lower it
      globally. \code{avoid\_collisions} is not the wearer check: the SRDF
      excludes the 44 proximal pairs a shoulder mount actually threatens, so a
      ``valid'' pose can have the tube inside the person. Measure clearance
      geometrically. The wearer's posture is part of the model and is a
      variable with one source; do not instruct a wearer to clasp their arms
      behind their back or hold them out to the sides.
\item \textbf{Anonymity is enforced in code.} The manifest writer raises on
      name, email, date of birth, address and phone.
\item \textbf{A demonstration is not evidence.} Demonstration clips carry that
      caveat in their caption and produce no trial data, no condition and no
      metric.
\end{enumerate}
