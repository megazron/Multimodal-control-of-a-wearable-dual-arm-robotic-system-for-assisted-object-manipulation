\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter is organised by theme rather than chronology. Five bodies of work
bear on the system built here: supernumerary limb design, the operator and
wearer split, master interfaces for dissimilar kinematics, shared autonomy,
and the measurement of embodiment and trust. The chapter ends by stating what
remains open, which is narrower than it first appears.

\section{Supernumerary robotic limbs}

The field's founding demonstrations are Parietti and Asada's bracing limbs for
aircraft fuselage assembly. Their limbs anchor against the environment to
stabilise the wearer's posture, and the same group later extended the
approach to whole-body support~\cite{parietti2016support}, which turns the wearer's body from a
disturbance source into part of a closed
chain~\cite{parietti2014bracing,parietti2013dynamic}. That framing matters
because it identifies the central mechanical problem early: an arm mounted on
a person exerts reaction forces into the person, and the person moves.

Reviews since have mapped the design space along consistent
axes~\cite{yang2021srlreview,prattichizzo2021augmentation,tong2021srlreview,zhang2023srlreview}.
Mounting location trades reach against the moment arm imposed on the wearer.
Actuation trades bandwidth against worn mass. Control input ranges from
residual body signals to explicit manual command. A more recent quantitative
survey adds a useful observation, that the field reports embodiment far more
often than it reports task performance, and rarely
both~\cite{zheng2026quantitative}.

Worn mass is the constraint that binds hardest and is discussed least. Each
Gen3 masses \SI{8.2}{\kilo\gram}, so the pair with harness exceeds
\SI{17}{\kilo\gram}. Simulation of the loads such systems impose on the wearer
suggests the biomechanical cost is not
negligible~\cite{moon2024impact}. \Cref{ch:discussion} returns to what this
implies for session design.

\section{The operator and the wearer as different people}

The configuration in this thesis has the arms worn by one person and commanded
by another. Fusion is the direct predecessor. It mounted a robotic head and
two arms on a worn frame and drove them from a remote
operator~\cite{saraiji2018fusion}. Two qualifications must travel with that
citation. Fusion's three modes, Direct, Induced and Enforced, are levels of
\emph{physical coupling} between the robot arms and the wearer's own arms, not
levels of machine autonomy; in all three the robot decides nothing. And the
publication is a two-page Emerging Technologies demonstration with no user
study and no performance numbers. It establishes that the architecture
existed. It cannot support any claim about how well it worked.

The evidence about how such systems are experienced comes from elsewhere.
Recent work on spatial guidelines for supernumerary limbs in near-body
interaction ran a concealed operator, varied the apparent autonomy, and
measured the wearer's physiological arousal and self-reported
trust~\cite{zhou2026proxemics}. The finding runs against intuition: higher
apparent autonomy made both worse, with skin conductance responses rising and
trust ratings falling. Their autonomy was a hidden human and their wearer had
no task of their own, so the result does not settle what happens when autonomy
is real and the wearer is occupied. It does establish that the wearer's
experience is not a free variable.

Disturbance rejection for the two-person case is partly solved already. Zhang
et al.\ compensate human-induced base motion for a supernumerary arm and
report end-effector errors of \SI{1.37(58)}{\milli\metre} with the human
shoulder as a floating base~\cite{zhang2024motioncomp}. What that work does
not cover is the case where the disturbance originates in a nervous system the
operator has no efference copy of. When wearer and operator are the same
person, the base motion is partly predictable to the controller because the
person commanding it also caused it. When they are different people, it is not.

\section{Master interfaces for dissimilar kinematics}

Where master and slave differ in scale or morphology, the mapping must be
chosen rather than assumed. Guggenheim and Asada argue for miniature physical
masters on the grounds that geometric similarity supplies feedback
inherently, without a separate haptic display~\cite{guggenheim2021haptic}.
The argument is persuasive for similarity of \emph{form}. It says nothing
about similarity of \emph{scale}, and scale is where the present system
departs: a ratio of roughly three to one between master and slave reach means
identical joint angles produce very different tip displacements.

Two mappings are available in principle. Joint-space correspondence sends
master joint angles to slave joint angles directly. It preserves the arm's
posture, which matters when the posture itself must avoid something, and it
requires every joint of the master to be measured accurately. Task-space
correspondence maps tip displacement to end-effector displacement, decoupling
the two chains at the cost of leaving the slave's redundant degree of freedom
unspecified. \Cref{ch:development} records why this project moved from the
first to the second.

Supplementary feedback is the other axis. Pinardi et al.\ review what
additional sensory channels do for control and embodiment in movement
augmentation, and find the two do not move
together~\cite{pinardi2023feedback}. Feedback that improves control does not
reliably improve the sense of ownership, which is a caution against treating
either as a proxy for the other.

\section{Shared autonomy}

The shared-autonomy literature addresses a different question from the
teleoperation literature: not how to transmit the operator's command, but how
much of it to obey. Dragan and Srinivasa formalise the arbitration as policy
blending, in which a predicted user goal and the user's actual input are
combined with a weight that depends on the confidence of the
prediction~\cite{dragan2013policyblending}. The framework is explicit that
blending too aggressively on a wrong prediction is worse than not blending at
all, which is the property that governs the arbiter built here.

Javdani et al.\ recast the problem as a partially observable Markov decision
process over the user's goal and approximate the intractable solution with
hindsight optimisation~\cite{javdani2015hindsight}. Their user study found
that assistance which does not wait for confidence to be high lets operators
finish faster with less input. The lesson taken here is narrower than the
method: an assistance policy needs an explicit representation of its own
uncertainty, and needs a defined behaviour for the case where the
distribution over goals is flat.

Neither line of work was developed for a robot mounted on a second person. In
both, the cost of assisting toward the wrong goal is a slower trial. Here it
is a manipulator moving toward someone who did not command it.

\section{Measuring embodiment, workload and trust}

Body ownership over an artificial limb is measurable, and the instruments are
long established. The rubber hand illusion showed that ownership can be
induced over an object that is plainly not one's
hand~\cite{botvinick1998rubber}, and Longo et al.\ decomposed the resulting
experience psychometrically into components including ownership, agency and
location~\cite{longo2008embodiment}. The decomposition matters for a
supernumerary limb because ownership and agency can move independently: an
operator may feel authorship of a motion without feeling the arm is part of
them, and a wearer may feel neither while still bearing the consequences.

Workload is conventionally measured with the NASA Task Load
Index~\cite{hart1988nasatlx}, which separates mental, physical and temporal
demand from effort, performance and frustration. For a two-person system the
index has to be administered to both people, and the two will not load the
same way. Physical exertion is better tracked with a Borg category-ratio
scale~\cite{borg1982perceived}, which is the appropriate instrument for the
operator's uncompensated arm and for the wearer's carried mass.

Interface design has its own standard. ANSI/ISA-101.01 sets out a philosophy
for operator displays in process
automation~\cite{isa101}, and its central principle transfers directly to a
robot console: colour is a scarce alarm channel, so a normal state should be
largely uncoloured rather than green. A display that paints five items red
simultaneously has communicated the same amount as one that paints none.

\section{The gap}

The gap is narrower than a first reading suggests, and stating it precisely
matters more than claiming it is wide.

Fusion established the operator and wearer architecture but measured
nothing~\cite{saraiji2018fusion}. Shared autonomy has been shown to raise
operator performance, in settings where the robot is not attached to
anybody~\cite{dragan2013policyblending,javdani2015hindsight}. Work on
near-body interaction has shown that increasing apparent autonomy lowers the
wearer's trust and raises their physiological
arousal~\cite{zhou2026proxemics}, in a setting where the autonomy was
simulated by a concealed human and the wearer had no task.

So it is not true that nobody has looked at any of this. What has not been
done is to measure both ends in one system: the operator's performance and the
wearer's experience, under the same autonomy, in the same trial. Until that is
done the exchange rate is unknown. If shared autonomy buys twenty per cent in
completion time and costs a point of wearer trust, that may be a good trade or
a bad one, and there is currently no basis for saying which.

The engineering contribution of this thesis is a system in which that question
could be asked. The measurement itself is not made here, and
\cref{ch:discussion} sets out what stands between the system as built and a
session with two people in it.
