\begin{abstract}
A supernumerary robotic limb is mounted on a person, so its base moves, is
soft, and cannot be commanded to hold still. This thesis reports a
teleoperation system for two seven-degree-of-freedom arms carried on a worn
backpack frame and commanded from an instrumented mannequin master, built on
two premises: that commanding fourteen joints through a body-worn master is a
usability problem rather than a plumbing one, and that a master routing
fourteen analog channels through rotating joints will lose channels, so losing
them must be survivable.

The command mapping takes each component of position from whichever sensor
observes it most directly, with elevation from the gravity vector, azimuth
from a single shoulder joint, and radius from a forward-kinematic magnitude
insensitive to roll errors. Channel health is classified per channel over
distinct sensor updates, validation is scoped to the channels the active mode
consumes, and the cost of each reduction is measured on \num{20430} recorded
frames. With no well-conditioned channels the system reports position
unavailable rather than extrapolating one.

Measurements on the physical master found six of fourteen channels usable and
reversed two long-standing verdicts, both of which had come from computing
statistics across transmitted rows rather than distinct updates. Vertical and
fore-and-aft command track correctly on both arms; lateral does not, and a
rotation-invariant test shows the measured directions are not an orthogonal
triad, so no axis map can correct them. Fourteen of fourteen injected faults
were handled, including two whose diagnosis required correcting the observable
a safety mechanism was keyed on.

The workspace characterisation returned a negative result with a quantified
mechanical remedy: under the as-built mount the two arms share no reachable
cell of a sixty-three-cell frontal grid, with a \SI{0.25}{\metre} dead band
between the reachable sets, which blocks inter-arm handover for geometric
reasons no control or planning change can address. A buildable mount
modification raising the shared count to twenty-two is quantified and
deliberately not applied.

No result was validated on a physical arm. The blockers to a two-person study
are enumerated, and the first is two soldered joints.
\end{abstract}
