\chapter{The vision pipeline, measured on the real cameras}
\label{ch:vision}

\emph{Measured on four physical cameras, 2026-08-30 -- together with
\cref{sec:hardware-session}, the only hardware numbers in this report.}

The pipeline is instrumented at thirty-three stages per camera; every stage
draws itself on the frame it was given, states its formula, and reports its
quantities with units, so a claim can be checked rather than taken on trust.
A stage has three possible outcomes, not two: it succeeds, it \emph{refuses}
and names its cause and remedy, or it is \emph{absent by design} (the
webcam's fifteen depth stages, because it has no depth sensor) -- rendering
an absence identically to a defect has cost this project findings before.

\section{The orientation defect}
\label{sec:orientation}

Three places in the software independently decided whether the scene depth
camera is mounted upside down, and disagreed. Settled by correlating each
frame's grey signature against the HD webcam (rotated by nothing, on the
same side of the room) in both orientations: the un-rotated frame agrees at
$r=\numrange{0.66}{0.70}$ over four captures, the rotated one at
$r=\numrange{0.27}{0.34}$ -- the same verdict every time, a factor of 2.4
apart. \textbf{The camera is mounted the right way up}, and every earlier
scene figure was inverted. Because the wrong rotation had been applied
\emph{consistently} to the image and the intrinsics together, the recovered
point cloud was an exact reflection of the true one and every distance,
extent and residual was preserved -- the defect was invisible to every
metric the pipeline itself computes and was found only by comparing one
camera's picture against another's.

\textbf{The wrist cameras measure a work surface to about
\SI{1.5}{\milli\metre}} -- within a factor of three of the same code against
a renderer, the first evidence the surface-fitting stage transfers to
hardware. \textbf{The scene camera does not identify a support plane}: its
best plane holds \SI{3.8}{\percent} of the points, so every quantity that
depends on it (height, object boxes) rests on a plane one point in
twenty-six agrees with. \textbf{No object in any frame could be grasped}:
the nearest object's narrowest axis measures \SI{144}{\milli\metre} against
an \SI{85}{\milli\metre} jaw. \textbf{The open-vocabulary detector returned
nothing on any camera} for the prompt \emph{green cube}; the classical
colour-and-geometry path found the same objects it did not.

Two defects in the first runs were software, not hardware. The pick path's
own depth working band, correct for a wrist camera, applied unchanged to a
camera across the room retained \textbf{0 of 85\,061 returns} -- the band is
now declared per source. And two stages disagreed about the same frame
because depth at $480\times270$ forward-projected into colour at
$1280\times720$ lands one sample in roughly every seven pixels, so the
above-plane mask was a dot pattern that failed a connectivity check while a
per-mask stage saw it fine; splatting each projected sample raised coverage
to \SI{64.4}{\percent} and \SI{58.8}{\percent}.

The arms were to be parked at the pick pose for this session; measured
rather than assumed, the left arm was \SI{10.67}{\degree} and the right
\SI{13.53}{\degree} from the reference configuration, against a
\SI{2}{\degree} tolerance, so no figure from this session is captioned "at
the pick pose."

\section{The scene the safety layer reasons about}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{vision/scene_boxes.pdf}
  \caption[The scene through the open-vocabulary detector]{The room camera
  frame through YOLO-World with the prompts \emph{mannequin, table, robot
  arm, box, cube}. Boxes and scores are the detector's own; two prompts
  returned nothing (legend). The figure referenced from
  \cref{ch:resultsmain}.}
  \label{fig:scene-boxes-full}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:scene-boxes-full} is the room camera's frame passed through the
project's own open-vocabulary detector, YOLO-World
(\repo{yolov8s-worldv2.pt}~\cite{cheng2024yoloworld}), asked by name for the
five classes the safety layer cares about: the mannequin the clearance
floor of \cref{sec:safety} is enforced against, the table, the robot arms,
the boxes and the cubes. Every rectangle is the detector's own output at a
\num{0.10} confidence threshold, drawn with its class and score; nothing is
hand-placed (\repo{thesis\_v3/figures/make\_scene\_boxes\_yolo.py}, output
also saved as \repo{scene\_boxes\_yolo.json}).

The result is instructive rather than flattering. The mannequin is found
twice, at \num{0.23} and \num{0.19}, the wider of the two boxes swallowing
the arms with the body; the table is found at \num{0.38} (and a second
time at \num{0.11} on the right-hand robot pedestal); a \num{0.35} ``box''
lands on the strip of task objects at the mannequin's waist. The two
wearable arms are not found at all under the label ``robot arm'', and no
cube is found at this distance -- the same failure mode as the
\SI{9.0}{\percent} recall of \cref{tab:vision-values} from the gripper
camera. Four of the five ``box'' detections are the pedestals of the two
industrial arms and the two tripods, not task objects. This is
why the shipped system never trusts a scene-camera detection for the
wearer: the clearance floor is enforced against the tracked body of
\cref{sec:safety}, with the mannequin as the fallback it can only enlarge.
