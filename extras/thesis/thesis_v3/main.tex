\author{Gaus Sayyad}

\begin{abstract}
A wearable robotic arm has a person as its base. This project built a
teleoperation system for two seven-joint robot arms carried on a backpack
frame and controlled by a \emph{second} person, gave it a safety layer
suited to a robot standing beside somebody who is not commanding it, and
ran a first pilot comparing direct control against shared-autonomy
assistance.

As mounted, the two arms could reach no common point; a costed mount change
fixed it and raised the clearance to the wearer from \SI{0.1610}{\metre} to
\SI{0.2202}{\metre}. Holding the wrist at a fixed orientation, not the
wearer's body, is what limits reach -- by a factor of five at the working
position. The task set solves \num{13927} joint-angle problems with no
failures; a new motion generator removed \SI{51.3}{\milli\metre} of
unrequested hand excursion; four real cameras find the table surface to
\SI{1.43}{\milli\metre}; and across every recorded session the automatic
clearance floor never engaged while the manual stop was used twelve times.

Under ethical approval (SETREC 7111986), five operators completed
twenty-three sessions comparing direct VR control against assistance:
assisted sessions were shorter and needed fewer re-grips, but the robot's
path was \SI{21}{\percent} longer while the operators' own hand paths were
within \SI{2}{\percent} of each other -- the extra distance comes from the
assistance, not the operator. The pilot has no separate wearer; nothing was
validated on a loaded physical arm; and the two-person study this system
was built for remains the target.
\end{abstract}
