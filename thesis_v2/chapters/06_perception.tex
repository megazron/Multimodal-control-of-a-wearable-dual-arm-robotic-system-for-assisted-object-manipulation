\chapter{Seeing, and deciding}
\label{ch:perception}

\section{The problem with written-down coordinates}

For most of this project the robot was told where things were. The blocks sat
at particular coordinates because a file said so, and the table was at a
particular height because a file said so. Every one of those numbers was
checked, repeatedly, along the whole path the arm would take.

None of it survives a different table. Or a different object. Or the same
object moved by five centimetres. The number in the file becomes a statement
the arm acts on with complete confidence, and there is nothing anywhere in the
system to disagree with it.

The parts needed to measure instead already existed and had never been
connected to each other. One module could find a flat surface and the things
standing on it, and was being used to print a description. Another could hold a
map of the space so the planner could avoid things that are not the wearer, and
had never been built outside its own self-test, which means no path this
project ever planned knew that the table was there. A third existed so the
surface height could come from the camera, and nothing called it.

\section{Looking like a printer, not like a person}

The first version of the sweep pointed the camera at each grid point in turn.
That seems sensible and it is wrong. Re-aiming means the wrist is at a
different angle in every frame, so any small error in where the camera sits
relative to the wrist becomes a \emph{different} error in every frame, and can
never cancel out or be measured.

A three-dimensional printer's height probe does not re-aim. It holds one
attitude and moves. The sweep does the same: one wrist direction per pass,
with the camera positions being the work positions shifted back along a fixed
line, so neighbouring views differ by a pure sideways move. Several passes at
different angles give the sides of objects, which a single viewpoint cannot
see. The full sweep is two heights, three angles, twenty-four positions and
two arms.

One more distinction turned out to matter. Being stationary is not the same as
having arrived. A joint can sit a small distance from where it was sent and
still be moving through that position, and a picture taken then is smeared
across two poses while every number downstream looks perfectly healthy. The
sweep now waits until no joint has moved by more than a tenth of the arrival
tolerance for four hundred milliseconds.

\section{Finding a particular object}

Detection uses an open-vocabulary detector that takes a phrase rather than a
fixed list of classes~\cite{cheng2024yoloworld}, with a fast segmentation
model to get the outline~\cite{zhao2023fastsam} and a plain colour-and-depth
filter as a fallback when neither model is loaded. Which of the three produced
a given detection is reported with the detection, so a result can never
quietly imply that the good one was running.

Two practical points are worth recording. Colour alone does not separate the
target from the laboratory: the green block and the teal robots on the far
bench differ by two units of hue, which is inside the noise. They are also
\SI{2.8}{\metre} away while the rig works within \SI{1.6}{\metre}, so distance
separates them cleanly and colour cannot. And the grasp itself is computed
geometrically from the shape of the depth points rather than learned, because
a two-fingered gripper closing on a rigid object is a solved problem, a
learned model would need graphics memory this machine does not have, and a
geometric method can explain why it refused.

That last property earns its place. Every refusal names a cause: no depth
data, wider than the gripper's \SI{85}{\milli\metre} opening, too few points to
fit a shape.

\section{Planning around things that are not the wearer}

For teleoperation there is no planner, and that is correct: the driver's hand
is the plan. For the autonomous mode there is a goal, and a planner was
written for it.

It searches in joint space rather than in the space the hand moves through,
because the thing that has to be avoided is the distance from the whole arm to
the wearer, not from the hand~\cite{kuffner2000rrtconnect}. It checks the same
clearance the safety layer enforces, rather than the simulator's collision
settings, for the reasons given in \cref{ch:safety}. It checks every
intermediate point along each candidate move at the same spacing the follower
uses, because a planner that checks only the ends of its moves has the same
defect it exists to remove. And it tries a straight line first, since most
moves are fine and planning a clear move turns sixty milliseconds into
several seconds.

Why this was needed can be stated in one measurement. A pick whose starting
pose and grasping pose are both individually clear, executed as a straight line
in joint space, passes within \SI{146}{\milli\metre} of the wearer at step
twenty-nine of sixty-four. The path checker catches it, and catching it means
the run stops.

\section{Taking an instruction in English}

The third way of driving takes a sentence. The parser is a grammar, not a
language model, and the reasons are given in \cref{ch:system}.

The important design decision is what happens when a sentence is ambiguous.
Asked to \emph{put the blue block on the blue mat} when there are two blue
blocks, the system asks which one rather than choosing. Nearest, leftmost and
first-detected are all defensible and all guesses, and this arm is bolted to a
person. Asked to \emph{put the blue ones on the blue mat}, it does not ask,
because the plural settles it. The difference lives in the words rather than
in a rule about how many things happen to match.

    \midrule

The bottom row is the one that matters. A refusal wastes the driver's time; a
misunderstanding moves the arm. All three misunderstandings in the earlier
version were compound instructions of the form \emph{put the blue ones on the
blue mat and the green ones on the green mat}, where the grammar planned the
first half, dropped the second in silence, moved two objects of four, and
reported success.
