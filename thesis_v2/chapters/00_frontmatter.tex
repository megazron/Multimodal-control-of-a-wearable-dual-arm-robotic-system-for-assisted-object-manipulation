\begin{abstract}
This project is about a pair of robot arms that a person wears on their back,
and about the problem of letting somebody else drive them safely.

The arms are two seven-jointed Kinova Gen3 manipulators on a backpack frame.
One person wears them. A second person, standing across the room, drives them.
That split is deliberate, and it changes the problem: the wearer cannot see
what the driver intends, cannot feel the controls, and did not choose the
motion happening beside their head. Everything in this thesis follows from
treating the wearer as a second participant rather than as part of the
apparatus.

Three ways of driving the arms were built and compared. The first is a
mannequin arm fitted with sensors, which the driver moves by hand. The second
uses a pair of hand controllers borrowed from a virtual reality headset, held
as motion trackers rather than worn. The third takes a sentence typed or
spoken in ordinary English and works out the rest. Around all three sits a
safety layer whose job is to keep the metal a fixed distance away from the
person carrying it.

Three findings shaped the outcome. The first is that the mannequin arm loses
sensor channels as its wiring flexes, so the software was built to keep
working with fewer of them, to say which ones it no longer trusts, and to
refuse rather than guess when too few remain. Seven of fourteen channels
currently fail the health test, and replaying the failing ones with the
driver's hand held still shows them injecting \SI{54.5}{\milli\metre} of
movement that nobody asked for.

The second is a limit of the machine rather than of the software. With the
arms mounted where they are, there is no point in front of the wearer's chest
that both arms can reach, and the innermost useful working column sits
\SI{325}{\milli\metre} to one side of the body centre line. That result was
reached four separate ways, including with the wearer's own arms deleted from
the model, and it rules out any task that needs the two hands to meet. A mount
change that would improve it is measured and costed, and deliberately not
applied.

The third concerns measurement itself. Over six weeks, a surprising result
turned out to be a fault in the measuring tool rather than in the robot on
seventeen separate occasions. The habit that came out of this, of clearing the
instrument before believing a bad number, is described in its own chapter with
the cases that produced it.

No result here has been validated on a moving physical arm carrying a load,
and no human data has been collected. The study protocol, the tasks, the
measures and the analysis are complete and are reported in full;
\cref{ch:participant} is deliberately left empty, with its tables and figures
in place, so it can be filled the moment ethical approval and a working
master arm allow the sessions to run.
\end{abstract}

\chapter*{A note on the words used}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{A note on the words used}

This thesis is written to be read by somebody who does not work on robots. A
small number of technical words cannot be avoided without making the sentences
longer than the ideas in them, so they are defined here once and then used
plainly.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[Words used in this thesis]{The technical vocabulary used in this
  thesis, in the sense meant here.}
  \label{tab:words}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.26\linewidth}p{0.68\linewidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Word} & \textbf{What it means here} \\
    \midrule
    Wearer & The person carrying the arms on their back. A participant, not
      equipment. \\
    Driver, or operator & The person commanding the arms. A different person
      from the wearer. \\
    Master arm & The mannequin arm fitted with sensors that the driver moves
      by hand. The robot copies it. \\
    Joint & One place where the arm bends or twists. Each robot arm has seven,
      plus a gripper. \\
    End effector & The point between the gripper's two finger pads, which is
      where the arm actually touches things. \\
    Pose & A position and an orientation together. Where the hand is, and
      which way it is pointing. \\
    Inverse kinematics & Working out the seven joint angles that put the hand
      at a wanted pose. There is usually more than one answer. \\
    Reachable & A pose the arm can adopt without hitting anything and without
      running out of joint travel. \\
    Clearance & The shortest distance from any moving part of the arm to any
      part of the wearer's body. \\
    Clearance floor & The smallest clearance the system will allow, set to
      \SI{150}{\milli\metre}. \\
    Simulation & A model of the arms and the wearer running on a computer,
      used to test motion before any metal moves. \\
    Node & One running program in the software. The system is built from about
      forty of them, each doing one job. \\
    Trial & One attempt at one task by one pair of people, under one way of
      driving. \\
    Dyad & One wearer and one driver, working together. The unit of analysis
      in the study. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two conventions are used throughout. Where a number is quoted, the program
that produced it is named, so it can be recomputed. Where a quantity has never
been measured, the text says so rather than offering an estimate, because a
figure with a guess inside it hides the thing that ought to be measured next.
