\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

Every result in this chapter is labelled with the strength of evidence behind
it, and the label is placed under the section heading rather than buried in
the text, so that no number can be read without it. Four classes are used and
the distinction is not cosmetic.

\begin{description}
  \item[Measured on physical hardware.] The numbers come from a physical
    device in the laboratory: the instrumented master arm, a moving Kinova
    arm, or a real camera. This is the strongest class and the rarest.
  \item[Measured on recorded hardware data.] The numbers come from data
    captured from a physical device and analysed offline. The device was
    real; the conditions were whatever they were on the day of capture.
  \item[Verified in simulation.] The numbers come from the simulated platform
    with mock hardware, using the same kinematic description, the same
    inverse-kinematics solver and the same safety code as the delivered
    system. Everything downstream of a joint command is modelled.
  \item[Measured on constructed data.] The numbers characterise an
    \emph{algorithm} against an input whose answer was chosen rather than
    observed. Legitimate for arithmetic and for statistics, never for
    perception, and always labelled as such.
\end{description}

\Cref{tab:evidence-map} places every result in this thesis in one of those
classes. \textbf{No result anywhere in this thesis was validated on a physical
Kinova arm carrying a load}, and the single session in which an arm moved at
all is \cref{sec:hardware-session}.

\section{The simulation campaign}
\label{sec:campaign}

\emph{Every stage in this section was run by one command on
2026-08-31. Nothing was copied from an earlier terminal.}

The measurements in this thesis were, until this point, each run once, by
hand, on the day the thing they measure was built. That is reproducible in
principle and not in practice: there was no such thing as ``the
simulations'' to run, only nineteen scripts with nineteen argument lists,
some needing a live motion planner and most not, and no record of which were
run together or against which geometry.

\repo{scripts/run\_simulation\_campaign.py} runs all of them, records what
each produced with its exit status and its duration, and writes one directory
this chapter is built from. \repo{scripts/make\_sim\_figures.py} turns that
directory into the tables and plots below; it \emph{refuses} to emit a table
whose stage did not run, so a missing result is visibly missing rather than
quietly stale. Both carry their own known-answer self-tests, including the
one that matters most here --- that a stage which could not run is reported
as failed rather than as absent.

\subsection{Computer vision, against a scene whose answer is known}
\label{sec:sim-cv}

\Cref{ch:vision} reports the vision pipeline on four physical cameras, where
the truth is whatever was in the room. Here the same code is run against a
constructed depth frame whose support plane, object pose, object size and
noise were all \emph{chosen}, so every stage can be scored rather than merely
observed.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[The vision pipeline against a constructed scene]{Twenty-one
  known-answer checks on a constructed frame: a plane at a chosen offset,
  a \SI{40}{\milli\metre} cube turned to a chosen yaw, and
  \SI{0.3}{\milli\metre} of noise. The rows marked \emph{control} are the
  half that makes this a measurement rather than a demonstration --- each is
  a naive method that must get the answer \emph{wrong}, and a change that
  broke the pipeline into agreeing with them would fail here.}
  \label{tab:sim-cv}
  \input{figures/sim/sim_cv_selftest.tex}
\end{table}

Three results follow, and the controls are what license them.

The geometry is exact where it should be exact. The fitted plane normal lands
\SI{0.0001}{\degree} from the constructed one and its offset within
\SI{0.005}{\milli\metre}; projection followed by deprojection returns the
original point to \SI{1e-17}{\metre}, which establishes that
\cref{eq:deproject} and its inverse are implemented consistently rather than
merely plausibly. The residual is \SI{0.151}{\milli\metre} on
\SI{0.3}{\milli\metre} of injected noise, which is the noise floor and not a
property of the fit.

The object measurement is accurate to under a millimetre in three dimensions.
The \SI{40}{\milli\metre} cube measures \SI{40.70}{\milli\metre} tall by
height above the plane, $\SI{40.8}{} \times \SI{40.7}{} \times
\SI{40.7}{\milli\metre}$ as an oriented box, and its centre lands
\SI{0.41}{\milli\metre} from the constructed one in full three dimensions.

\textbf{And the two obvious shortcuts are measured to be wrong, by how much.}
An axis-aligned bounding box on the same turned cube reads
\SI{56.54}{\milli\metre} --- \SI{41}{\percent} too wide, which on this
platform is the difference between a grasp the \SI{85}{\milli\metre} jaw
accepts and one it refuses. Taking the object centre as the mean of its
visible points is out by \SI{16.15}{\milli\metre} along the surface normal,
because a single view sees a shell and not a solid; the correction that
assumes the object rests on the fitted plane brings that to
\SI{0.35}{\milli\metre}. Both errors are systematic, both are invisible in a
rendered picture, and both are terms in the budget of \cref{ch:motion}.

\subsection{Teleoperation: what the smoother does to the operator's hand}
\label{sec:sim-smoothing}

Measured through the shipped node rather than against the filter in
isolation: a stationary master carrying \SI{1.5}{\milli\metre} RMS of tremor,
and a \SI{0.40}{\metre\per\second} reach, at \SI{50}{\hertz}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[Master smoothing]{Stillness and lag, measured together. They are
  the pair a fixed coefficient cannot win: the tremor is high frequency at
  low amplitude and the motion is low frequency at high amplitude, so any
  single cutoff trades one against the other. The fixed exponential average
  the node ran until 2026-08-27 buys
  \SI{1.5}{\milli\metre} of stillness for \SI{18.7}{\milli\metre} of lag ---
  more than half the capture gate, spent on a hand that is simply moving.}
  \label{tab:sim-smoothing}
  \input{figures/sim/sim_teleop_smoothing.tex}
\end{table}

The speed-adaptive filter is steadier \emph{and} less laggy than the one it
replaced: \SI{0.83}{\milli\metre} against \SI{1.10}{\milli\metre} when still,
and \SI{1.59}{\milli\metre} against \SI{18.67}{\milli\metre} when moving.
That is not a better setting of the same trade; it is off the fixed filter's
trade-off curve, which is what an adaptive cutoff buys and the only reason to
pay for one.

That measurement is on constructed signals, which is the right input for
establishing that the law does what its equations say, because the answer is
known before the filter runs. It is not what a hand does: a person's tremor
is not white, their reaches are not ramps, and the two are interleaved at a
cadence nothing in the constructed test reproduces. The same two quantities
were therefore measured again over \num{20440} frames of a recorded session
with a person actually driving the master. The input is a real recording;
what is simulated is the rest of the pipeline, run offline over it.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[The smoother over a recorded session]{The same two quantities on a
  real operator's hand: \num{20440} recorded frames per arm over
  \SI{755}{\second}. Still and moving segments are found once, from the
  \emph{raw} commanded position, and the same segmentation is applied to
  every filter --- deriving it from each filter's own output would let a
  heavier filter classify more of the recording as still, which is the

  \label{tab:sim-replay}
  \input{figures/sim/sim_teleop_replay.tex}
\end{table}

The ordering survives contact with a real hand and the margins do not. On
recorded data the adaptive filter is again steadier than the fixed one on
both arms and again costs a fifth of its lag --- \SI{2.13}{\milli\metre}
against \SI{10.78}{\milli\metre} on the left, \SI{3.53}{\milli\metre} against
\SI{10.27}{\milli\metre} on the right --- but the stillness gain is a tenth
of what the constructed test showed. The reason is visible in the
segmentation: a hand that a person is holding still is not a stationary point
plus noise, it drifts, and a filter that removed the drift would be removing
the operator. The constructed test overstates what smoothing can buy because
it constructs a stillness that a person does not have.

One number in that table is not about smoothing at all. The recording carries
\num{20440} frames over \SI{755}{\second}, which is \SI{27.1}{\hertz} of
\emph{distinct} commanded poses against a serial frame emitted at
\SI{100}{\hertz}. Three quarters of the frames the master sends carry a pose
it has already sent. \Cref{ch:master} gives the candidate mechanism --- the
firmware prints an integer, so a slow channel crosses a printable boundary
only occasionally --- and records that it has not been isolated.

\subsection{Shared autonomy: what the intent estimator is actually sensitive to}
\label{sec:sim-shared}

The shared-autonomy layer's whole function is to answer \emph{which object is
the operator reaching for}. Its inputs are the object positions, so its
accuracy can be no better than the scene description it is given. In
simulation the declared coordinates \emph{are} the truth, so a
declared-position estimator and a perfectly calibrated one receive identical
inputs and score identically --- which is a result about the renderer and not
about the system. The declared error is therefore swept, over the range a
real table actually spans, and the calibrated arm is given the error the
calibration \emph{achieves} (\SI{11.3}{\milli\metre}) rather than a hoped-for
zero.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/sim/sim_autonomy_sweep.tex}
  \caption[Intent inference against a mis-declared table]{Intent inference
  over \num{300} trials per point, four candidate objects on a
  \SI{60}{\milli\metre} pitch. The quantity that matters is not the correct
  rate but the \textbf{confidently wrong} rate, because a wrong answer held
  with confidence is the one state in which this system assists an operator
  toward an object they were not reaching for. Working from declared
  coordinates, it reaches \SI{50}{\percent} once the declaration is one cube
  pitch out. Working from measured ones it stays at \SI{0.0}{\percent}
  across the whole sweep, and the correct rate stays flat at
  \SIrange{71.7}{75.7}{\percent}.}
  \label{fig:sim-autonomy}
\end{figure}

The shape of the two curves is the argument for the perception stage of
\cref{ch:perception}, stated as a number rather than as a principle. A
declared-coordinate system does not degrade gracefully as the room drifts
from the file: it becomes confidently wrong, and it becomes so at a
displacement of one object pitch, which is a table nudged by a hand. The
measured-coordinate system is flat because the quantity it depends on ---
\SI{11.3}{\milli\metre} of calibration error --- does not grow when the table
moves.

There is a second reading, and it is less flattering. The correct rate never
exceeds \SI{76}{\percent} in \emph{any} condition, including the one where
the estimator is given perfect information. The remaining quarter is
ambiguity, not error, and the arbiter is specified to stay in direct
teleoperation whenever it is ambiguous. So the honest summary of this figure
is that calibration converts a failure mode that moves the arm into one that
does not, rather than that it makes the estimator right more often.

Correct interpretations rose from \num{21} to \num{34} of \num{75}, refusals
fell from \num{37} to \num{27}, and misunderstandings fell from \num{3} to
\num{0}. Ten of the fourteen questions the system asks carry a scored reply,

everything must not be able to look like one that understands everything.

The figure to read is the last pair of bars. A system that refuses is
annoying and a system that misunderstands is dangerous, and the change bought
both: fewer refusals \emph{and} no misunderstandings, rather than trading one
for the other.

\emph{Verified in simulation, against a live motion planner.}

Four stages need a live motion planner, so they were run in a second pass

planning clients against one scene has already produced a measurement in
which one run's furniture was present in the other run's collision world, and
the resulting figure looked like a task failure.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[The task set inside the simulated platform]{Every commanded
  coordinate of the task set, solved against a live motion planner. Paths are
  densified to \SI{20}{\milli\metre} and each pose is solved $N$ times,
  because the solver restarts randomly and a pose that solves once has not
  been shown to be reachable. \emph{Floor breaches} counts poses in which any

  pairs a shoulder mount actually threatens.}
  \label{tab:sim-tasks}
  \input{figures/sim/sim_task_set.tex}
\end{table}

\num{13927} inverse-kinematics calls, zero failures, zero clearance breaches.
Three things about that number are worth stating, because a clean table is
the easiest kind to over-read.

\textbf{It reproduces.} This is the second consecutive campaign to return the
same result field for field, which is the property this project spent a long
time not having: earlier reachability figures were re-asserted rather than
re-measured, and \cref{ch:instrument} records two occasions on which a clean
sweep was measured with the arms somewhere other than where the task
commands them.

\textbf{The controls fired.} Every stage in this pass carries poses that must
be refused as well as poses that must be accepted --- a target
\SI{1.6}{\metre} out, a target inside the wearer's torso, a target inside the
bench, and for T1 a clearance probe that must go from clear to buried when a
slab is added. All were refused. A reachability sweep with no unreachable
control is a sweep that cannot fail.

\textbf{It says nothing about grasping.} Reaching every commanded coordinate
is a statement about the arm, not about the hand. The same run measures the
grasp itself from forward kinematics on the finger tips: the cube presents
\SI{56.3}{\milli\metre} across the closing axis against an
\SI{85}{\milli\metre} jaw, the pads land \SIrange{9.57}{9.58}{\milli\metre}
from the declared coordinate, and the follower supplies a \SI{5.0}{\degree}
wrist tilt to make the pose reachable at all --- a tilt that costs
$2|\mathbf{r}_{\text{pad}}|\sin(\theta/2) = \SI{8.58}{\milli\metre}$ of pad
displacement by construction, and \SI{9.58}{\milli\metre} as measured. That
is inside the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} capture gate and it is not zero, and
\cref{ch:motion} treats it as a term in the budget rather than as a rounding
error.

\subsubsection{What the pinned wrist costs, and what its own control caught}

The fourth stack stage measures the reach available under each orientation
policy, walking outward in fourteen directions from each arm's home pose with
the \SI{150}{\milli\metre} wearer floor enforced throughout. It is the stage
that took four runs to produce a number, and the sequence is reported in full
because the diagnosis at the end of it is worth more than the number at the
end of it. Every one of the four is in \cref{tab:sim-orientation}: the table
is generated from the campaign's own logs, so a run that was superseded stays
in the record rather than being replaced by the one that came after it.

The instrument carries a monotonicity control. The poses a \emph{tighter}
orientation policy admits are a subset of the poses a looser one admits, so a
looser policy can never reach less far. If it does, the boundary was drawn by
the solver rather than by the arm.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption[Reach against orientation policy, at each sampling density]{The
  same measurement at every seed count it was run at. Mean reach over
  \num{28} directional walks, by how tightly the commanded wrist is
  constrained; the \emph{pinned} row is what teleoperation actually runs.
  Generated from the campaign's own logs, so a further run adds a column
  rather than replacing one --- which is the point, because the finding here
  is the difference between the columns.}
  \label{tab:sim-orientation}
  \input{figures/sim/sim_orientation_convergence.tex}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Three seeds was under-sampled, and it would have contradicted an
established result.} The first run reported six walks in which a looser
policy reached less far. Between three seeds and ten the pinned mean moved
\SI{52}{\milli\metre}, more than a tenth of the quantity being measured. The
consequential difference is elsewhere in the table: at three seeds, freeing
the wrist roll appears to buy \SI{38}{\milli\metre} of mean reach; at ten it
buys \SI{3}{\milli\metre}. Earlier work in this project measured the roll to
be worth nothing and refused it by name, on the grounds that a fixed
six-degree-of-freedom pose on a seven-degree-of-freedom arm spends the whole
redundancy whether or not the roll is free. The under-sampled run agreed with
neither that measurement nor that argument --- and it printed a full table to
three decimal places while doing so.

\paragraph{Ten seeds and twenty agree, so the estimate is converged.} Three
of the five policies are identical to the millimetre between them and the
other two move by \SI{5}{\milli\metre}. Whatever else is true, the sampling
is no longer what limits this measurement.

\paragraph{And the control still failed --- on exactly one walk, with
byte-identical numbers at both seed counts.} That reproducibility is the
diagnosis. A stochastic artefact does not survive doubling the sampling
unchanged; a deterministic one does. The failing comparison was
\texttt{pinned \num{0.900} > spin \num{0.875}} on the right arm's
$(-1,-1,+1)$ walk, and \SI{0.900}{\metre} is the \emph{search cap}: that walk
ran to the end of the search without ever being refused, so its reach is a
lower bound and not a boundary. Comparing a lower bound against a boundary is
not a monotonicity test, and it was never going to pass. The instrument's own
advice --- \emph{raise the seeds and re-run} --- was wrong for that one case,
and following it cost two runs of half an hour each.

\paragraph{The control was corrected rather than the number.} Capped
comparisons are now excluded, \emph{counted, and named}: the one comparison
the instrument declines to make is printed beside the ones it makes, with the
walk and the values that caused it, because silently dropping the comparisons
a control finds inconvenient is how a control stops being one. Relaxing a
control is exactly the kind of change that needs pinning, so
\repo{test\_the\_reach\_control\_still\_catches\_a\_real\_violation.py} holds
both halves: an uncapped violation must still fail, a capped \emph{looser}
policy must \emph{not} excuse one --- only the tighter side being a lower
bound makes the comparison meaningless --- and a set of reaches that is
entirely capped must produce exactly the capped skips and no more.

The last column of \cref{tab:sim-orientation} is that run, and the property
worth noticing is that \textbf{every reach in it is identical to the column
beside it}. The correction changed the instrument's \emph{verdict} and not
its \emph{measurement}, which is what distinguishes a control that was wrong
from a threshold that was moved until the answer became acceptable. Had any
number shifted, the correction would have been the second kind.

With the control corrected the stage passes, and two results follow.

\textbf{Freeing the wrist buys reach, and the roll buys almost none of it.}
Position-only commanding reaches \SI{87}{\milli\metre} further on average
than the delivered pinned policy, and a \SI{45}{\degree} cone
\SI{71}{\milli\metre}; the roll alone is worth \SI{3}{\milli\metre}, which
independently reproduces the earlier finding that the roll is not where the
redundancy goes.

\textbf{Orientation freedom does not buy anything at the boundary that
matters.} Six of \num{28} walks stop because of the wearer rather than
because the arm ran out of reach, and that count does not fall as the policy
loosens --- it is six under every policy from pinned to position-only.
Loosening the wrist lets the arm go further in directions where the wrist was
the limit, and does nothing whatever in directions where the limit is a
person. That is the operationally important half, it is robust across every
seed count in \cref{tab:sim-orientation}, and it is the reason
\cref{ch:workspace} treats the mount rather than the control policy as the
thing that would have to change.

\section{Command mapping}

\subsection{Axis correctness}

\emph{Measured on recorded hardware data, and verified in simulation.}

Regressing commanded pose against the master's physical spherical position
over all sweep segments, at \num{6443} samples with $R^2 = \num{0.999}$,
returned a gain matrix of $\mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1)$ against the intended
identity. The pipeline was faithfully implementing an axis map that was wrong:
raising the master drove the arm down and moving it forward drove the arm
back.

The correction is not a free choice of sign. In spherical form the elevation
convention already carries the vertical sign, so any axis map with a negative
vertical entry double-negates it, and no such map can produce correct
behaviour. After correction, raising the master by \SI{30}{\degree} moves the
arm up.

\subsection{The lateral axis remains wrong, and the axis map cannot fix it}

\emph{Measured on recorded hardware data.}

Up and down and fore and aft track correctly on both arms. Lateral does not.

Fitting the best rotation from measured sweep directions to intended
directions leaves a residual of \SI{54.8}{\degree} RMS on the left arm and
\SI{54.7}{\degree} on the right. A rotation-invariant test explains why no
rotation will help: intended left-right and forward-back are
\SI{90}{\degree} apart, and measured they are \SI{137.7}{\degree} apart. A
rotation preserves angles, so no rotation and no axis permutation maps one
triad onto the other. The measured directions are not an orthogonal triad.

The mechanism is a lever arm, and identifying it correctly took two attempts.
Joint one is the first joint and its axis is fixed in the mount, so it does
not tilt as the arm raises. What changes is the offset of the tip from that
axis: rolling joint one with a straight arm moves the tip by exactly zero, and
produces lateral motion only once a downstream bend offsets the tip. Azimuth
per degree of joint one therefore scales with how bent the arm is, so azimuth
and elevation are not independent, which is precisely what the spherical model
assumes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/tikz/lateral.tex}
  \caption[The lateral constraint]{Two master motions intended to be
  orthogonal arrive \SI{137.7}{\degree} apart at the commanded end effector.
  The test is independent of any axis labelling, which is what makes this a
  geometric limitation rather than a calibration error.}
  \label{fig:lateral}
\end{figure}

Replacing azimuth with the forward-kinematic horizontal angle was measured and
made things worse, from \SI{12.6}{\degree} to \SI{33.3}{\degree} mean
reversal error on the left arm. Gyroscope-derived azimuth halves the residual
on the left arm, from \SI{25.6}{\degree} to \SI{13.5}{\degree}, and is
implemented but not enabled: the right sensor drifts at
\SI{44}{\degree\per\minute} while stationary, and three directions from one
segment each does not establish a triad.

\subsection{Indexing}

\emph{Verified in simulation, against the synthetic serial device.}

Ten index cycles gave a re-engagement jump of \SI{0.29}{\milli\metre} mean and
\SI{0.35}{\milli\metre} maximum, drift while disengaged of
\SI{0.000}{\milli\metre}, and total travel of \SI{338.8}{\milli\metre} over
six cycles with the second half still advancing. The reachable set is bounded
by the robot, not by the master's range.

The first run of this measurement showed four consecutive cycles of zero
travel, which was a real defect rather than a measurement artefact: the radial
fallback had integrated to its clamp, so further wrist deflection did nothing
and said nothing. The clamp now warns by name. This is the silent-blocking
class exactly, and the fix is not to remove the clamp but to make reaching it
audible.

\section{Safety}

\subsection{Fault injection}

\emph{Verified in simulation, fourteen injected faults.}

All fourteen were handled: channel dropout, board disconnect, board
reconnection, emergency stop during motion, wrong-object latching, ambiguous
target flicker, stale object, detection failure, logging failure, identifying
data in a manifest, non-monotonic clock, session loss, zero camera detections,
and network dropout.

Two of these took several attempts and the reasons are transferable. The
dead-man did not trip on ten seconds of total master silence, which was
diagnosed at the time as a broken dead-man and was not. The master republishes
stale data at full rate, so the topic never went silent; the mechanism was
keyed on the wrong quantity. And the emergency stop's named block did not
appear in the aggregated view precisely when the dead-man fired, because the
follower set that blocker only inside its pose callback, which stops being
called at the moment the master goes quiet.

\subsection{An emergency stop that was four seconds late}

\emph{Verified in simulation.}

Detection was always on time. The halt was not. The trip handler called the
real-driver shutdown first, which waited on two services that do not exist on
a simulated stack, so both timed out in full inside the node's own timer.
Measured: decision at \SI{0.97}{\second}, trip logged at

park them, and the log line that would have revealed this was itself behind
the block.

The fix parks first, using only local publishes, and makes the driver path
non-blocking.

\section{Grasping}

\emph{Verified in simulation.}

The grasp sequence executes a pre-grasp standoff with the wrist pre-aligned, a
straight approach along the approach vector, closure to the object's width,
retreat along the same vector, transit, and release at the placement. Every
candidate is validated at the standoff, at the grasp, and at every point of
the approach between them before it is offered.

Two defects were found in the recorded gripper trace rather than by
inspection, and neither was visible in the source.

Two components were writing the gripper on the same cycle. The recorded
knuckle angle sawed through the lift phase, from \num{0.42} to \num{0.18} to
\num{0.24}, and reached \num{0.05}, fully open, at the start of transit: the
object was grasped, dropped, and re-grasped in mid-air when the second
component's schedule crossed its own threshold. After giving the gripper a
single owner, the lift phase holds \num{0.424} with minimum equal to maximum
over eighteen samples, and there is exactly one release, at the placement.

The object marker attached on a band rather than on the object's width, so a
\SI{40}{\milli\metre} block requiring a knuckle angle of \num{0.424} attached
at \num{0.157}, with the fingers still visibly open. Attachment now requires
\num{0.90} of the width-specific target and occurs at \num{0.399}.

\subsection{Top-down grasping has a lateral boundary}

\emph{Verified in simulation.}

Top-down grasp candidates fail entirely at $|x| = \SI{0.25}{\metre}$, at zero
of nine, and succeed entirely at $|x| = \SI{0.30}{\metre}$, at nine of nine.
The failure persists with collision checking disabled, so it is wrist
orientation infeasibility and not obstruction. Every pick point in the task
set is placed beyond that boundary as a result.

\section{The wrist cannot be aligned under teleoperation}

\emph{Verified in simulation, and a limitation rather than a result.}

The default orientation mode pins the commanded wrist to the anchor captured
at the home pose. For the task-set block at $(0.32, 0.35, 1.15)$, the
top-down grasp requires a rotation of \SI{169.7}{\degree} from that anchor,
which is very nearly a reversal.

There is no channel to command it. The master's wrist is unmeasurable, with
left j7 restricted to \SI{26}{\degree} and j6 incoherent, and right j3, j5 and
j7 dead or incoherent, which is why the mode defaults to pinned. Orientation
assist, which exists to supply it, is a stub because the solver plugin ignores
orientation constraints.

The recorded grasp sequences work because the recorder calls the
inverse-kinematics service directly with the grasp orientation, bypassing the
teleoperation orientation lock. They demonstrate that the \emph{robot} can
execute an aligned grasp. They do not demonstrate that an \emph{operator} can
command one, and the three recorded conditions differ only in trajectory
smoothing, so they do not distinguish this either.

This is the one place where autonomy earns a categorical rather than a
quantitative advantage over direct teleoperation on this platform, and it
should be claimed exactly that narrowly.

\section{Motion generation}

\emph{Measured offline against the platform's own forward kinematics.}

The per-joint step limiter that constituted the entire motion generation for
teleoperation leaves the commanded end effector \SI{51.3}{\milli\metre} off
the path its own solver solution implies on the left arm, and
\SI{39.2}{\milli\metre} on the right, against a grasp capture gate of
\SI{30}{\milli\metre}. It commands peak joint speeds of \num{12.53} times the
manufacturer's declared limit, because it had never read the limits file. Both
figures fall to zero and to \num{1.00} respectively under the jerk-limited,
phase-synchronised generator that replaced it. \Cref{ch:motion} gives the
diagnosis, the metric that had to be corrected before the comparison meant
anything, and the five defects found in the replacement.

\section{The environment as measured rather than declared}

\emph{Verified against the simulated scene and against mock depth.}

The calibrate-then-plan stage of \cref{ch:perception} recovers six objects
from a full sweep with every centre within \SI{1}{\milli\metre} in both
horizontal axes and every extent within \SI{3}{\milli\metre}, and measures the
support height to \SI{1.25026}{\metre} against a declared
\SI{1.250}{\metre}. Coverage is \SI{98.65}{\percent} of the cells the arm can
reach, with no stillness refusals, in thirteen minutes.

Two properties matter more than the accuracy. The map is the first structure
in this project that a motion planner has consulted: before it, the planner's
only collision object was the wearer, so a path that routed correctly around a
person swept straight through the bench. And the map carries the time it was
made, and the pick stage refuses to move from a map older than thirty minutes,
because a stale record is indistinguishable from a fresh one by inspection and
the failure it produces is the arm driving confidently at coordinates measured
in a previous session.

\section{Instruction handling}
\label{sec:instruction-results}

\emph{Measured on constructed scenes; grounding rather than detection.}

Seventy-five phrasings of the pick-and-place instruction, scored against the

\section{The first hardware session}
\label{sec:hardware-session}
\label{sec:hardware}

\emph{Measured on a physical arm. This is the only section of this thesis
whose measurements come from a moving Kinova arm, and none of them is a
performance result.}

A single arm was driven from its own wrist camera for the first time. The
grasp geometry was never the difficulty; the session went almost entirely to
instruments that produced confident wrong answers, seven of which are recorded
in \cref{ch:instrument}. Three findings survive as results.

\paragraph{The camera-to-wrist transform is wrong by roughly
\SI{8.5}{\degree}, and the error rotates with the wrist.} The table cannot
move, so the surface normal measured from any arm pose must be the same
vector. Measured from four poses it read \SI{9.74}{\degree},
\SI{15.42}{\degree}, \SI{16.26}{\degree} and \SI{18.14}{\degree} from world
vertical, a worst pairwise disagreement of \SI{8.45}{\degree}. That is
\SI{34}{\milli\metre} of position error at \SI{0.23}{\metre} and
\SI{59}{\milli\metre} at \SI{0.40}{\metre}. It is the term
\cref{fig:budget} marks as unmeasured, and it is the reason no open-loop
grasp planned through that transform landed inside the capture gate.

\paragraph{The depth sensor itself is excellent and was never the problem.}
The support plane fits at \SI{94.8}{\percent} inliers with
\SI{0.46}{\milli\metre} residual, and a \SI{40}{\milli\metre} calibration cube
measures $39.2 \times 38.9 \times \SI{40.9}{\milli\metre}$. Only the transform
out of the camera frame is in error, which is what makes closed-loop servoing
in the camera's own frame the correct response: a closed loop converges
through a wrong extrinsic and an open loop cannot.

\paragraph{The middleware is a first-class hazard on this host.} Two transport
faults cost more of the session than any robotics problem. The default
communication domain's shared memory was in a state where no \emph{new}
process could join it while every already-running node continued normally: a
minimal publisher and subscriber delivered zero of three messages on that
domain and three of three on another. And an unset middleware implementation
variable caused the client library to discover nothing at all while the
process graph was plainly visible to other tools. Both present as an
unresponsive robot.

\Cref{ch:discussion} treats what this session implies for the readiness of the
platform, and \cref{ch:conclusion} places the calibration of that transform
second in the order of work.

\section{What is not measured}

The following are implemented and unmeasured, and none should be read as
working.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anything derived from a force reading.} The mock hardware
    returns identically zero for every force, so payload compensation,
    interaction force and grip force at the slave are \emph{structurally
    undefined} rather than merely unmeasured. Comparing tracking with and
    without a payload configured returned a difference of exactly
    \SI{0}{\metre}, which is a property of the mock and not evidence.
  \item \textbf{Object detection rate at working distance.} The models fit in
    the available video memory at \SI{2012}{\mebi\byte} of \SI{4096}{\mebi\byte},
    but the synthetic renderer used to measure detection produces images
    outside the detector's training distribution. The same detector scores
    \numrange{0.89}{0.91} on a real photograph and zero on the synthetic task
    objects, so the measurement characterises the renderer. The detection gate
    is neither passed nor failed.
  \item \textbf{Grasp success against a physical object.} The autonomous
    modes execute end to end in simulation; on hardware the sequence servos
    but no grasp has yet closed inside the capture gate, for the transform
    reason in \cref{sec:hardware}.
  \item \textbf{End-to-end latency including the arm.} Every latency reported
    above is control-path latency within one host and is a lower bound.
  \item \textbf{How the terminal joint error varies with commanded speed.}
    Reported previously as measured and retracted in \cref{ch:motion}: the
    three speeds were three replicates of one condition.
  \item \textbf{Speech recognition against a human speaker.} The host exposes
    no audio input device, so every voice figure in this project was produced
    from synthesised speech and characterises the grammar rather than the
    recogniser.
  \item \textbf{Two arms driven simultaneously on hardware.} Measured at
    \SIrange{8.1}{12.2}{\hertz} with send latency spiking to
    \SI{519}{\milli\second} against \SI{21.0}{\hertz} for one arm, which is
    past the bridge's own watchdog, so the joints stop while still being
    commanded. The per-arm rate limit that follows from this is a hypothesis
    derived from the latency and has not been tested.
\end{itemize}
