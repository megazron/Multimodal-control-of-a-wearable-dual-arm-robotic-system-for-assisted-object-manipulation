\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

A supernumerary robotic limb is an extra arm worn on the body. It works
alongside the wearer's own limbs rather than replacing or rehabilitating
them~\cite{yang2021srlreview,prattichizzo2021augmentation}, and the same
idea serves an assistant at a workbench, a surgeon who needs a third hand,
or a person with limited mobility. What makes such a device different from
a fixed industrial robot is simple but consequential: the base of the robot
is a person. It accelerates, sways and steps, and it cannot be told to hold
still.

This thesis reports work on Imperial College London's Dr.\ Octopus platform
(the MUlti-limb Virtual Environment~\cite{muve}), which mounts two Kinova
Gen3 robot arms~\cite{kinova2022gen3}, each with seven joints, on a backpack
frame. The arms are controlled by a \emph{second} person: an operator who
cannot feel the arms, steering them for a wearer who cannot command them.
No previous study has measured, in one system and in the same trial, both
what automated assistance gives such an operator and what it costs the
person carrying the arms (\cref{sec:gap}). Closing that gap is what this
project was built for.

Two decisions shaped the engineering: the operator's hand position is read
from whichever sensor sees each part of it best, and a lost sensor is a
normal condition the system announces rather than hides -- next to a
person's head, a made-up position is worse than none.

\section{Background}
\label{ch:background}

\textbf{Supernumerary robotic limbs.} Parietti and Asada's founding
demonstrations brace an extra limb against the environment to steady the
wearer~\cite{parietti2014bracing,parietti2013dynamic}. Later reviews map the design choices along consistent axes -- where the
limb is mounted trades reach against leverage on the wearer, how it is
driven trades speed against carried weight -- and note that the field
reports how the limb \emph{feels} far more often than how well it
\emph{performs}~\cite{yang2021srlreview,prattichizzo2021augmentation,tong2021srlreview,zhang2023srlreview,zheng2026quantitative}.
Weight is the tightest limit: each Gen3 weighs \SI{8.2}{\kilo\gram}, the
pair with harness exceeds \SI{17}{\kilo\gram}, and simulated loads suggest
the cost to the wearer's body is not negligible~\cite{moon2024impact}.

\textbf{Operator and wearer as different people.} Fusion mounts a robotic
head and two arms on a worn frame driven by a remote
operator~\cite{saraiji2018fusion}, but its modes are degrees of
\emph{physical} coupling, not machine autonomy, and no user study is
reported. Work on robots close to the body found that raising
\emph{apparent} autonomy -- a hidden human operator, a wearer with nothing
to do -- raised physiological arousal and lowered
trust~\cite{zhou2026proxemics}, so the wearer's experience matters. The
two-person case also differs mechanically: compensating a single wearer's
own body motion reaches \SI{1.37 \pm 0.58}{\milli\metre} of hand
error~\cite{zhang2024motioncomp}, but there the disturbance comes from the
operator's own nervous system, which knows in advance what it is about to
do; here it comes from somebody else's.

\textbf{Controllers shaped unlike the robot.} Guggenheim and Asada argue
that a miniature controller of similar shape gives the operator an
intuitive feel for the robot~\cite{guggenheim2021haptic}; the argument
covers \emph{shape}, not \emph{size}, and this controller is a third the
robot's size. Copying joint angles needs every controller joint measured
accurately; copying hand position does not, but leaves the robot's spare
seventh joint undirected.

\textbf{Shared autonomy.} Dragan and Srinivasa formalise shared control as
a blend of what the operator commands and what the robot predicts, weighted
by the robot's confidence~\cite{dragan2013policyblending}; Javdani et al.\
treat it as a decision under uncertainty about the operator's goal and show
that assisting before the robot is certain still helps operators finish
faster~\cite{javdani2015hindsight}. Neither was developed for a robot on a
second person, where a wrong guess sends a manipulator toward someone who
did not ask.

\textbf{Measuring embodiment, workload and trust.} A person's sense that an
artificial limb is theirs is measurable and separates into ownership,
agency and location~\cite{botvinick1998rubber,longo2008embodiment};
workload is measured by the NASA Task Load Index~\cite{hart1988nasatlx},
exertion by a Borg scale~\cite{borg1982perceived}. Feedback that improves
control does not reliably improve ownership~\cite{pinardi2023feedback}, so
performance and embodiment must be measured separately; the pilot here
measures the first and claims nothing about the second.

\section{The gap}
\label{sec:gap}

No study measures the operator and the wearer together, under the same
autonomy, in the same trial. Shared autonomy is known to help an operator
when the robot is attached to nobody; apparent autonomy is known to lower a
wearer's trust when the autonomy was a hidden human. Until both are
measured in one system, the exchange rate between what assistance gives the
operator and what it costs the person carrying the arms is unknown. The
full review is in \cref{app:background}.

\section{Aim, objectives and hypotheses}
\label{sec:aim}

The aim was to build and verify, on the platform of \cref{fig:platform}, a
two-person teleoperation system on which that exchange rate can be
measured, and to begin measuring it. Six objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Design and build an instrumented mannequin controller (the
    \emph{master}) with the robot's joint layout at reduced scale, and
    characterise it against recorded data.
  \item Derive a mapping from master pose to robot command that degrades
    predictably as sensors are lost, and quantify what each loss costs.
  \item Implement a safety architecture suited to a robot mounted beside a
    person, validated by deliberately injecting faults.
  \item Measure the reachable workspace of the mounted arms and which
    manipulation tasks it can support.
  \item Establish a verification method that produces traceable evidence
    for every claim, and apply it throughout.
  \item Run a pilot of direct control against shared-autonomy assistance
    with real operators, and identify what stands between it and the full
    two-person study.
\end{enumerate}

The study the system was built for states five hypotheses, each with a
predicted direction, before any data (\cref{app:study}): task completion
time is shorter under shared autonomy (H1); operator workload is lower
(H2); wearer-reported trust is \emph{lower} under shared autonomy (H3),
opposite to H1 and H2; the wearer's sense of agency falls while ownership
does not (H4); and interference between the two arms falls while the delay
between them does not (H5). The pilot tests H1 directly; no workload
questionnaire was administered, so H2 is untested, and H3--H5 need the
separate wearer the pilot did not have.

Each objective is reported in \cref{ch:resultsmain} with the strength of
its evidence -- implemented, verified in simulation, measured on hardware,
or measured in the pilot. The planning report's force-feedback master was
not delivered (\cref{ch:discussionmain,app:development}). \textbf{No result
below the pilot was validated on a physical arm carrying a load.}
