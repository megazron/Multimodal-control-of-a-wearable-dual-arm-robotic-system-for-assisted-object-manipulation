\subsection{Computer vision against a constructed scene}
\label{sec:sim-cv}

Twenty-one known-answer checks on a frame whose plane, object pose and size
were chosen. The fitted plane normal lands \SI{0.0001}{\degree} from the
constructed one; a \SI{40}{\milli\metre} cube measures $40.8 \times 40.7
\times \SI{40.7}{\milli\metre}$ and centres to \SI{0.41}{\milli\metre}. Two
naive shortcuts are measured wrong by name: an axis-aligned bounding box
reads \SI{41}{\percent} too wide, and taking the object centre as the mean
of its visible points is out by \SI{16.15}{\milli\metre} along the surface
normal (a single view sees a shell, not a solid) -- corrected to
\SI{0.35}{\milli\metre} by assuming the object rests on the fitted plane.

\subsection{Shared autonomy: what the intent estimator is sensitive to}

Sweeping the declared-position error over the range a real table spans, a
declared-coordinate estimator becomes \emph{confidently wrong} \SI{50}{\percent}
of the time once the declaration is one object-pitch out; a measured-coordinate
one stays at \SI{0.0}{\percent} across the same sweep, with the correct rate
flat at \SIrange{71.7}{75.7}{\percent}. The correct rate never exceeds
\SI{76}{\percent} in any condition, including perfect information -- the
remaining quarter is ambiguity, and the arbiter is specified to stay in
direct teleoperation whenever ambiguous.

\subsection{Full autonomy: seventy-five phrasings}

Correct interpretations rose from 21 to 34 of 75 against an earlier grammar
revision, refusals fell from 37 to 27, misunderstandings fell from 3 to 0.
Ten of fourteen clarifying questions carry a scored reply; all ten resolve
correctly.

\section{Axis correctness}

Regressing commanded pose against the master's physical spherical position
(\num{6443} samples, $R^2=\num{0.999}$) found a gain matrix of
$\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1)$ against the intended identity: raising the master
drove the arm down. Corrected. The lateral axis remains wrong and cannot be
fixed by any axis map: measured directions meant to be \SI{90}{\degree}
apart are \SI{137.7}{\degree} apart, and a rotation preserves angles, so no
rotation maps one triad onto the other. Gyroscope-derived azimuth halves the
residual on the left arm (\SI{25.6}{\degree} to \SI{13.5}{\degree}) but is
not enabled: the right sensor drifts at \SI{44}{\degree\per\minute}
stationary.

\section{Safety: fault injection and timing}

Fourteen of fourteen injected faults handled: channel dropout, board
disconnect/reconnect, emergency stop mid-motion, wrong-object latching,
ambiguous-target flicker, stale object, detection failure, logging failure,
identifying data in a manifest, non-monotonic clock, session loss, zero
detections, network dropout. Dead-man timing against a \SI{1.00}{\second}
deadline: healthy master, 243 frames, no trip; \textbf{degraded (publishing
at full rate, frozen source timestamp): trip at \SIrange{0.94}{0.99}{\second}}
-- the case that could not have passed before keying the dead-man on the
source timestamp; clean unplug: trip at \SI{0.97}{\second}. A separate
emergency-stop defect: the trip handler called a blocking real-driver
shutdown first, so decision at \SI{0.97}{\second} produced trip \emph{logged}
at \SI{4.98}{\second} -- fixed by parking first, from local publishes only.

The Kortex figure constrains the architecture: the cyclic control interface
needs a \SI{1}{\kilo\hertz} loop on a dedicated link, and over this
machine's virtualised network each cyclic write costs about
\SI{10}{\milli\second} -- at \SI{100}{\hertz} the write alone consumes the
cycle budget and no command reaches the arm while telemetry keeps arriving.
The high-level velocity interface avoids this because it holds a commanded
motion between sends.

\section{The first hardware session}
\label{sec:hardware-session}
\label{sec:hardware}

\emph{Measured on a physical arm -- the only section of this chapter whose
numbers come from a moving Kinova arm.} A single arm was driven from its own
wrist camera for the first time. Three findings survive as results.
\textbf{The camera-to-wrist transform is wrong by roughly \SI{8.5}{\degree}
and rotates with the wrist}: the surface normal measured from four poses of
a stationary table read \SI{9.74}{},\SI{15.42}{},\SI{16.26}{},\SI{18.14}{\degree}
from vertical, a worst pairwise disagreement of \SI{8.45}{\degree} --
\SI{34}{\milli\metre} at \SI{0.23}{\metre}, \SI{59}{\milli\metre} at
\SI{0.40}{\metre}. \textbf{The depth sensor itself is excellent}: the
support plane fits at \SI{94.8}{\percent} inliers, \SI{0.46}{\milli\metre}
residual, and a \SI{40}{\milli\metre} calibration cube measures $39.2 \times
38.9 \times \SI{40.9}{\milli\metre}$ -- only the transform out of the camera
frame is wrong. \textbf{The middleware was a first-class hazard}: shared
memory in a state where no new process could join while running nodes
continued normally, and an unset middleware variable caused silent discovery
failure. Both present as an unresponsive robot.

\section{What is not measured}

Anything derived from a force reading (the mock returns identically zero);
object detection rate at working distance (the renderer used to measure it
is out of the detector's training distribution); grasp success against a
physical object; end-to-end latency including the arm and operator; how the
terminal joint error varies with commanded speed (retracted in
\cref{ch:motion}: three speeds were three replicates of one condition);
speech recognition against a human speaker (no audio input device on this
host; every voice figure characterises the grammar, not a recogniser); two
arms driven simultaneously on hardware (measured at \SIrange{8.1}{12.2}{\hertz}
with send latency spiking to \SI{519}{\milli\second}, past the bridge's own
watchdog).
