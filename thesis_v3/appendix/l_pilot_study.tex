\chapter{The pilot session, in full}
\label{app:pilot}

\noindent\emph{\Cref{sec:pilot-results} reports the headline pilot numbers.
This appendix is the full record: coverage, every session, and the
supplementary measures.}

Twenty-three sessions were recorded, sixteen on 2026-09-03/04 for P1--P4 and
seven on 2026-09-02 for P5 (\repo{recordings/sessions/}). Duration, the
robot's end-effector path and lag come from the full-resolution position trace
(20 Hz); clutch and emergency-stop counts come from each session's event
log. The session folders were named at the console, and the names mixed
the two tasks up: sessions named "object tracking" were the pick-and-place
task, and "position matching" or "position reaching" was the same
target-reaching task under another name. The operator who ran the sessions
confirmed the mapping, and every table and figure here uses the two real
task names (\repo{figures/relabel\_pilot\_tasks.py}). P5's sessions predate the per-participant folder-naming convention
(generic timestamp names, no task label) and are identified as direct or
shared from the \texttt{shared\_autonomy} flag each session's own
\texttt{events.jsonl} logs at start, which -- unlike the folder names relied
on for P1--P4 -- is populated correctly for every one of P5's sessions.
Participant identity is P1--P5 throughout, including in every figure pixel,
consistent with \repo{write\_manifest()} refusing to record a name
(\cref{sec:ethics}).

\section{What the gripper record says about pick and place}
\label{sec:gripper-record}

The \SI{20}{\hertz} trail never logged a gripper command (its
\repo{grip\_cmd\_*} columns are empty in every session), so whether a pick
happened was read from two other records (\cref{tab:gripper-use}): the
controller's index trigger, which is what the live gripper node closes the
hand from, passed through that node's own latch; and the physical gripper's
measured finger position from the bag. The two agree. P1 closed the gripper
eight times and held it closed for \SI{18}{\second} in total, P2 four times
for \SI{5.8}{\second}, and P5 four times for \SI{133}{\second} -- carrying
the object; P3 closed it once, to \num{0.90} of full travel, for
\SI{0.7}{\second}; P4 never closed it in any session. That is the record
behind the statement in \cref{sec:pilot-results}: three pick-and-places
completed, one lift, one reach. P5's task labels were never recorded, and
the two long holds identify which of P5's sessions were the pick-and-place
attempts.

\section{Sim-to-real tracking during the pilot}

Every pilot session polled the physical Kinova arms' own joint encoders
alongside the simulated joint state, which makes a direct check possible:
does the real arm actually follow the simulated one it was commanded
through. \Cref{fig:track} is the best-tracking session across all twenty-three
(P4, pick and place, direct control, chosen by lowest sim-to-real joint
RMS): the real arm's joint 4 angle follows the simulated one to
\SI{1.06}{\degree} RMS over \num{3859} samples, with the largest departures
during the fastest motion and near-zero error while the arm holds still.
This is the same terminal-offset mechanism \cref{sec:sim-to-real} measures
from replayed commands, seen here live during an operator session rather
than in an offline replay -- \textbf{it is evidence the sim-to-real bridge
was live and tracking throughout the pilot}, not a claim that any pilot
result was validated against a loaded arm, which requires the arm to be
carrying and moving an object rather than only mirroring a joint angle.

\section{The master-arm cohort}

Three further operators (M1--M3, disjoint from P1--P4) attempted the same
protocol from the instrumented mannequin master rather than VR controllers,
across sixteen further recording attempts on 2026-09-02 and 2026-09-04.
\textbf{Seven of those sixteen are confirmed empty}: \texttt{ros2 bag info}
reports zero messages, or no bag metadata at all, for every attempt at the
multimeter task and both of M1's target-reaching attempts -- the same
recording-pipeline failure this project's own session index for that day
logged independently (eight of twenty-six recordings clean, ten with no data
at all). The nine that did record are real sessions,
\SIrange{72}{304}{\second} each, tens of thousands of messages.

M1's tracking error is not noise: it is consistent across all three of that
operator's sessions and separated from M2 and M3 by a factor of five to
fifty. The mechanism this project's own channel-health measurement predicts
(\cref{ch:results}) is a session that leans on the incoherent left-arm
channels rather than the coherent right-arm ones -- all three of M1's
sessions used the \emph{right} arm as the active hand (\cref{tab:master-sessions}),
so a simple left/right split does not explain it on its own, and which
specific channels M1's sessions exercised has not been isolated. It is
reported here as a finding this pilot surfaced, not a diagnosis: the
master-arm modality can apparently produce sim-to-real tracking an order of
magnitude worse than either VR teleoperation or another operator on the same
hardware, on the same day, and the pooled VR-only comparison of
\cref{sec:pilot-results} would have missed it entirely.

\paragraph{Limits.} Five participants with uneven task coverage is a
pilot-scale sample: no significance test is reported, and none of the
differences above should be read as a proven effect. The pooled figures mix
the two tasks together; the paired panels are the part of this pilot closest
to the paired design of \cref{app:study}. Session duration runs from the
start of the recording to the end, including any explanation or hesitation,
so it is a session-length measure rather than a pure motor-execution time.
Every session's emergency stop was engaged once, near the end, to stop the
recording -- a procedural marker rather than a safety incident -- and is
excluded throughout. This pilot has no separate wearer: every measure here
is what the operator experienced, and the wearer-side hypotheses of
\cref{app:study} (H3, H4) remain untested.
