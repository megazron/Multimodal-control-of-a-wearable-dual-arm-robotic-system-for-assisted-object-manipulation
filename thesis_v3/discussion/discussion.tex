\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussionmain}

This project built and verified a teleoperation system for two robot arms
worn by one person and controlled by another, and ran a first pilot of
direct control against shared-autonomy assistance with real operators. Its
main findings: the two arms as mounted could reach no common point, and a
costed mount change fixed it; the safety layer's own pilot record shows the
automatic clearance floor was never needed; and assistance shortened
sessions and reduced re-grips while lengthening the robot's path.

\section{The workspace result, and what it costs}

The finding that the arms shared no reachable point is severe because it is
geometric: no planner or wrist policy can produce a point neither arm can
reach, which ruled out hand-to-hand transfer. The cause was not the mount's rotation but the arms' asymmetric rest
angles, which are fixed facts because the simulation-to-hardware bridge
replays them onto the real arm. Fixing it needed a mechanical change,
costed, applied and re-verified in one pass.

\section{The safety layer is thorough about distance and silent about time}

The clearance floor is a \emph{distance}, and the wearer can move.
Combining the measured delay of the wearer-tracking camera with limb speeds
from the literature gives \cref{tab:reaction-compact}: in most modelled limb
motions the limb crosses the whole \SI{150}{\milli\metre} margin before the
robot knows anything changed. It is this report's principal open safety
question, and three
responses exist, none yet implemented: enlarge the floor by the delay times
an assumed limb speed, a one-parameter change that makes the margin mean
what it claims; cut the delay, since most of the \SI{562}{\milli\second}
tail is a configured publishing rate, not the \SIrange{28}{34}{\milli\second}
the tracker itself takes; and scale commanded speed with measured
clearance, two quantities the system already computes separately. The
fallback makes it worse: when the wearer estimate is stale the guard
reverts to a mannequin with its arms down and believes there is
\SI{0.3170}{\metre} of clearance where a wearer with arms held out has
\SI{0.0933}{\metre} -- optimistic by \SI{224}{\milli\metre}, mitigated only
by instructing the wearer.

\section{Validity of the measurements}
\label{sec:validity}

Twenty-six times in this project a measurement was wrong and the system was
not (\cref{ch:instrument}). Fifteen were caught because an independently known
quantity disagreed with the reading, and fourteen could have been caught in
advance from something already in hand -- the case for building a
cross-check into every instrument rather than trusting it harder. Every
result other than the pilot and the sensor measurements comes from
simulation with mock hardware, whose force readings are exactly zero, so no
force-based quantity is defined here; and every delay reported excludes
operator, display and physical arm, so it is a lower bound.

\section{What the pilot shows, and its limits}
\label{sec:pilot-limits}

The pilot points the same way as the shared-autonomy literature for robots
attached to nobody~\cite{dragan2013policyblending,javdani2015hindsight}:
shorter completion times and fewer re-grips with assistance, and no
measurable cost in responsiveness. The robot's path is \emph{longer} under
assistance, in the paired comparison too (six of seven cases) -- but the
operators' own controller paths are not: \SI{29.9}{\metre} against
\SI{30.5}{\metre}, within \SI{2}{\percent}. The extra distance therefore
belongs to the assistance, and the platform shows the same with no operator
at all: identical scripted waypoints travel \SI{4}{\percent} further under
VR shared autonomy than direct VR, and \SI{45}{\percent} further under
mannequin-master shared autonomy than mannequin direct
(\cref{fig:mode-travel}). The literature predicts the cost this
pilot could not see: higher autonomy improves performance but lowers the
sense of agency~\cite{collier2025agency}, cuts effort without raising
satisfaction~\cite{kim2012autonomy}, and raises safety while lowering
perceived control~\cite{you2011assisted}; no agency or workload
questionnaire was administered here, which is why H2 is untested and H4 is
in the protocol. Five operators with uneven coverage is a pilot, not a
powered comparison: the full study's pre-registered analysis, run on
\num{20000} constructed data sets (\cref{app:analysis}), detects a medium
effect ($d_z=\num{0.73}$) with \SI{97.3}{\percent} power across all tasks
at sixteen pairs, but on any one \emph{named} task with only
\SI{63.8}{\percent}; \SI{80}{\percent} needs twenty pairs, the sample the
next study should recruit. Most importantly, the pilot has no wearer: it
measures what the operator experienced, not what carrying
\SI{17}{\kilo\gram} of assisted motion costs a second person -- the
comparison \cref{app:study} is for.

\section{Against the planning report}

The planning report specified physical springs at the master and a virtual
spring-damper at the robot; neither survives the measured system, whose
usable command path runs at \SIrange{18.4}{18.7}{\hertz}, too slowly for a
spring-damper law to behave as one. The compliance sought would have to
come from the mechanism -- a spring in series with each
motor~\cite{toubar2022sea,lee2019sea} or current-based
control~\cite{dewolde2024impedance} -- both hardware changes. The safety,
sensor-degradation, workspace and pilot work were not in the plan; each
followed from a measurement the plan did not anticipate.

\section{Against the objectives, and what remains}
\label{sec:against-objectives}

Of the six objectives of \cref{sec:aim}, five were met and one partly: the
master built and characterised, its repair narrowed to two joints (1); a
mapping that degrades predictably (2); a safety layer that handled fourteen
of fourteen injected faults and whose live record shows the automatic floor
was never needed (3); a workspace characterisation that
found no shared reachable point and costed, applied and re-verified the fix
(4); ten-repeat, known-answer verification that caught twenty-six
instrument defects before any became a finding (5); and a pilot under
approval that supports H1 at pilot scale but, with no workload
questionnaire and no wearer, leaves H2--H5 untested, so objective 6 is met
in its first half only.

What remains, in priority order and with the number that sets it: repair
the two named master sensors (\SI{54.5}{\milli\metre} of unrequested
motion); measure the camera's true position on the wrist
(\SIrange{34}{59}{\milli\metre} of error, the largest unmeasured term and
the reason no camera-guided grasp has closed on hardware); correct the
clearance check that samples link origins rather than link geometry, the
one safety defect here rather than an incompleteness (\cref{ch:discussion});
and run the protocol of \cref{app:study} with a separate wearer, now that
approval and a working input path both exist.
