\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:resultsmain}

Every number below states the strength of its evidence, and \textbf{no
result before the pilot in \cref{sec:pilot-results} was validated on a
physical arm carrying a load}: most of this project is simulated against
mock hardware, plus recorded data from the physical master and four cameras
(\cref{ch:results}: full derivations).

\section{The command mapping and its safety layer}
\label{sec:safety}

Six of fourteen master sensors are alive (\cref{sec:channelhealth}; the
graceful-degradation design is discussed in \cref{sec:degradation-appendix}).
The safety layer handled fourteen of fourteen injected faults
(\cref{sec:channelhealth}).

The pilot (\cref{sec:pilot-results}) is the only \emph{live} evidence for
this layer (\cref{fig:estop-timeline}): across every recorded session the
automatic clearance floor -- the rule that holds an arm \SI{150}{\milli\metre}
short of the wearer -- never activated. The manual emergency stop did:
twelve holds across eleven of twenty-three sessions, nine of them in a
session's final \SI{15}{\percent} as the operator ended the recording, and
three not -- one assisted session stopped at \SI{67}{\percent} and
\SI{83}{\percent} of its length, with steering resuming after each.

\section{Workspace and motion}

Over a 63-cell grid across the working volume in front of the wearer,
\textbf{no cell was reachable by both arms} (\cref{fig:disjoint}).

  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\linewidth}

    \label{fig:disjoint-b}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption[The disjoint workspace, and the mount change that fixes it]{
  (\subref{fig:disjoint-a}) The two arms' reachable regions do not meet.
  (\subref{fig:disjoint-b}) Sweeping the mount position: tilting forward
  and moving inboard help, splaying outward \emph{hurts}. Geometry only, so
  these are upper bounds pending a clearance check.}
  \label{fig:disjoint}

Moving the mounts inboard, forward and down with more forward tilt raises
shared cells to 22 of 63 within a buildable \SI{0.20}{\metre} separation;
applied, it raised clearance at the rest pose from \SI{0.1610}{} to
\SI{0.2202}{\metre} and moved the pick-and-place targets from \SI{0.530}{}
to \SI{0.290}{\metre} off centre. The tightest limit on reach is the fixed
wrist orientation: from the working position, the hand can travel a mean
\SI{0.065}{\metre} with the wrist held fixed against \SI{0.363}{\metre}
with it free -- over five times -- because a fixed orientation uses up the
seventh joint (\cref{tab:work-point}).

The per-joint step limiter that was the whole motion generator is compared
with its replacement in \cref{tab:motiongen-main}: it let the hand leave the
planned straight line by nearly twice the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} grasp gate,
at over twelve times the declared joint speed, because it never read the
speed-limit file.

Replaying recorded commands on the physical arm shows every joint stops
\SI{0.305}{\degree} short of its target on the side it came from -- a fixed
stop-short, not an error growing with distance ($r=\num{0.923}$ when
propagated to hand position). One correction per arm removes
\SIrange{74}{76}{\percent} of it, taking the combined positioning error
from \SI{18.64}{} to \SI{12.91}{\milli\metre} (worst case \SI{33.39}{} to
\SI{19.99}{\milli\metre}), inside the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} requirement
\emph{provided} the camera sits where the model says on the wrist. Measured,
it does not: \SI{8.45}{\degree} and \SIrange{34}{59}{\milli\metre} of error
across the working range (\cref{sec:hardware-session}).

\section{Perception: what the cameras actually detected}

Pointed at four physical cameras for the first time (\cref{ch:vision}), the
wrist cameras find the table surface to \SI{1.43}{\milli\metre} with
\SI{74}{\percent} of their depth points on it (plane fitting by random
sample consensus~\cite{fischler1981ransac}). Segmentation is scoped to the
task's two objects (\cref{fig:vision-pipeline}), recovered as oriented boxes
so the planner sees a turned object's true width.

No grasp closed on any camera in that session: the nearest object's
narrowest side measured \SI{144}{\milli\metre} against an
\SI{85}{\milli\metre} gripper opening. \Cref{fig:scene-boxes-full} shows
the whole scene the safety layer reasons about.

In simulation the full task set solves \num{13927} joint-angle problems
with no failures and no clearance breaches, and every scripted grasp closes
on its object in every mode (\cref{fig:grasp-grid}); the cubes are set down
\SI{56}{\milli\metre} from the centre of their pad, past the
\SI{30}{\milli\metre} gate (\cref{fig:pick-frames}). The intent estimator
-- the part of shared autonomy that guesses which object the operator wants
-- shows why measuring matters: from a typed-in table position it guesses
the wrong object with confidence \SI{35}{\percent} of the time at one cube
spacing (\SI{60}{\milli\metre}) and \SI{17.5}{\percent} at
\SI{40}{\milli\metre}, past the \SI{16}{\percent} error rate above which
aggressive assistance is a net loss~\cite{dragan2012formalizing}; from the
camera's measured position, \SI{0.0}{\percent}.

\section{A pilot of direct control against shared autonomy}
\label{sec:pilot-results}

\textbf{Five operators (P1--P5)} completed twenty-three recorded sessions
under the protocol of \cref{sec:ethics}, each on one of three tasks --
\emph{object tracking} (following a held, moving object with the robot's
hand), \emph{target reaching} (a reach to a marked position) and
\emph{position matching} (moving to and holding a target pose) -- once
under direct control and once with assistance. P5's seven unlabelled
sessions are pooled, their condition read from the logged flag. A fourth
planned task, using a multimeter, was never recorded with a participant on
any of three tries (\cref{app:pilot,app:gallery}). Coverage is uneven, so the
pooled comparison mixes tasks and the paired one isolates the condition.

Pooled across all twenty-three sessions (\cref{tab:pilot-summary},
\cref{fig:pilot-time-a}), assisted sessions averaged shorter completion
times (\SI{219}{\second} against \SI{241}{\second}) and fewer re-grips
(\num{23.3} against \num{25.8}), with the robot lagging the hand by under
\SI{2.9}{\milli\metre} in both conditions -- but a \emph{longer} robot path
(\SI{2.78}{\metre} against \SI{2.30}{\metre}), a direction that holds in
the paired comparison too (\cref{fig:pilot-variance},
\cref{sec:pilot-limits}). \Cref{fig:paired-slopes} isolates the seven cases
in which the \emph{same} operator repeated a task under both conditions:
the robot travels further with assistance in six of seven, the operator's
own hand in only two of seven.

Every session also recorded the real arm's own joint encoders, so the gap
between the simulated and the real hand is known throughout

mannequin cohort, most sessions inside the \SI{30}{\milli\metre} grasp gate.
