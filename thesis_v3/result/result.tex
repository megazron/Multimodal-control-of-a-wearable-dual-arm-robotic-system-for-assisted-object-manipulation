\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:resultsmain}

Every number below carries its strength of evidence, and \textbf{no result
before the pilot in \cref{sec:pilot-results} was validated on a physical
Kinova arm carrying a load}: most of this project is simulated against mock
hardware, plus recorded data from the physical master and four cameras
(\cref{ch:results}: full derivations).

\section{The command mapping and its safety layer}
\label{sec:safety}

Six of fourteen master channels are alive; the per-channel verdicts, the
\SI{54.5}{\milli\metre} of unrequested motion the incoherent ones inject,
and the repair that follows from it are moved to \cref{sec:channelhealth}
in full, alongside the degradation architecture's own discussion
(\cref{sec:degradation-appendix}) -- this section carries only what the
safety layer itself did, live, on the pilot below.

The safety layer handled fourteen of fourteen injected faults (channel
dropout, emergency stop mid-motion, wrong-object latching, stale
detections, clock non-monotonicity and more); the two that needed several
attempts both traced to a mechanism keyed on the wrong observable, fixed by
reading the master data's source timestamp rather than its arrival, and by
parking the arm before talking to the driver rather than after.

The pilot (\cref{sec:pilot-results}) is the only source of \emph{live}
evidence for this layer: across all twenty-six recorded sessions the
geometric clearance floor -- the mechanism that would hold an arm
\SI{150}{\milli\metre} short of the wearer -- never activated once, meaning
no recorded operator ever drove either arm inside that margin. The
manual/dead-man emergency stop did fire for real: four holds across three
sessions, one tripping twice, the first held \SI{22.6}{\second}
\emph{mid}-session (\SI{67}{\percent} through) with clutch engagement
resuming around it -- not an end-of-recording gesture. The geometric path
remains validated only against synthetic fault injection.

\section{Workspace and motion}

Over a 63-cell grid spanning the frontal working volume with both arms
verified at the home pose, \textbf{no cell was reachable by both arms}
simultaneously (\cref{fig:disjoint}), and the result survives freeing the
wrist yaw and adding solver retries.

Moving the mounts inboard, forward and down with extra forward tilt raises
shared cells to 22 of 63 within a buildable \SI{0.20}{\metre} separation.
That change was applied, raising home-pose clearance from
\SI{0.1610}{\metre} (a reading of the mount itself, since no joint moves
it) to \SI{0.2202}{\metre}, and moving pick-and-place targets from
\SI{0.530}{\metre} off centre to \SI{0.290}{\metre}. Neither arm crosses
the body centre line at any surface height or distance in \num{3360} swept
configurations; the constraint is the wearer's torso, not arm posture.

The per-joint step limiter that constituted the whole of teleoperation
motion generation, measured against a \SI{30}{\milli\metre} grasp capture
gate, is compared with its jerk-limited replacement in
\cref{tab:motiongen-main}: it left the hand off the solved path by nearly
twice the gate, at over twelve times the manufacturer's declared joint
speed, because it never read the limits file.

Replaying recorded joint commands on the physical arm shows every joint
parks \SI{0.305}{\degree} short of its target, on the side it approached
from -- a terminal offset, confirmed by propagating the error through the
manipulator Jacobian ($r=\num{0.923}$) rather than a proportional-gain
model. A single scalar per arm, applied as overshoot in the direction of
travel, removes \SIrange{74}{76}{\percent} of it. Folded into the
positioning error budget with a re-derived pad offset and a
measured-surface centring correction, this takes the combined budget from
\SI{18.64}{} to \SI{12.91}{\milli\metre} RSS and \SI{33.39}{} to
\SI{19.99}{\milli\metre} worst case -- inside the \SI{30}{\milli\metre}
requirement, \emph{provided} the still-unmeasured camera-to-wrist transform
is small. The first sustained attempt to drive a physical arm from its own
wrist camera measured that transform directly and found it is not: four
poses of a stationary table disagreed by \SI{8.45}{\degree}, \SIrange{34}{59}{\milli\metre}
of position error over the working range (\cref{sec:hardware-session}).

\section{Perception: what the cameras actually detected}

Instrumented at thirty-three stages per camera and pointed at four physical
cameras for the first time (\cref{ch:vision}), the wrist cameras fit a
support plane at \SI{1.43}{\milli\metre} RMS with \SI{74}{\percent} inliers
at working distance -- within a factor of three of the same code against a
renderer, the first evidence the surface-fitting stage transfers to
hardware. Segmentation is scoped to the two objects the task actually uses,
not the whole scene (\cref{fig:vision-pipeline}): the cube is isolated by
the project's own colour classifier, and the box -- outside that palette --
by thresholding on its own measured hue, since applying the cube's palette
to it misfires on the box's brown tones. Both recover as oriented boxes
rather than axis-aligned ones, which is what makes a turned object's true
width -- the quantity the grasp planner needs -- available at all.

  wrist camera's raw frame (a), the cube and box segmented from it (b), and

  every stage, not three different examples.}
  \label{fig:vision-pipeline}
\end{figure}

Depth, not colour, is what a box is drawn from (\cref{fig:depth-boxes}):
the same two objects, boxed and range-labelled in their own depth map.
\Cref{tab:vision-values} collects this frame's numbers against the
plane-fit method of \cref{fig:vision-pipeline}; the two disagree by tens of
millimetres because they measure different points on a tilted object.

No grasp closed on any camera this session: the nearest object's narrowest
axis measured \SI{144}{\milli\metre} against an \SI{85}{\milli\metre} jaw, a
statement about the scene rather than the planner. \Cref{fig:scene-boxes-full}
places this against the safety layer's own subject in one frame: wearer,
table, the wearable arms and the objects between them.

In simulation, the full task set solves \num{13927} inverse-kinematics
calls with zero failures and zero clearance breaches. Sweeping the
shared-autonomy intent estimator against a mis-declared table shows why:
from declared
coordinates it becomes \emph{confidently wrong} \SI{50}{\percent} of the
time once the declaration is one object-pitch out; from measured
coordinates it stays at \SI{0.0}{\percent} across the same sweep.

\section{A pilot of direct control against shared autonomy}
\label{sec:pilot-results}

\textbf{Five operators (P1--P5)} completed twenty-three recorded sessions
under the protocol of \cref{sec:ethics}, each on one of three VR-teleoperated
tasks -- \emph{object tracking} (following a moving held object with the
end-effector), \emph{target reaching} (a discrete reach to a marked
position) and \emph{position matching} (driving to and holding a target
pose) -- once under direct control and once with shared-autonomy assistance.
P5's seven sessions predate the per-task naming convention and are pooled
as one unlabelled block, identified as direct/shared from the session's own
logged flag. A fourth, planned task using a multimeter as the manipulated
object was attempted by the master-arm cohort below but never recorded on
any of its three tries (\cref{app:pilot}). Coverage is uneven by design --
not every operator performed every task under both conditions in the time
available -- so the pooled comparison below mixes tasks together and the
paired comparison that follows it is the part of the data that isolates the
condition cleanly. Full session-by-session data, the coverage table, and
the supplementary measures are in \cref{app:pilot}.

Pooled across all twenty-three sessions (\cref{tab:pilot-summary},
\cref{fig:pilot-time-a}), shared-autonomy sessions averaged shorter
completion times (\SI{219}{\second} against \SI{241}{\second}) and fewer
re-grips per session (\num{23.3} against \num{25.8}), with a robot lag under
\SI{2.9}{\milli\metre} in both conditions -- but \emph{more} hand-travel
distance (\SI{2.78}{\metre} against \SI{2.30}{\metre}), a direction that
holds in the paired comparison too (\cref{sec:pilot-limits}), not just the
pooled one (\cref{fig:pilot-variance}). \Cref{fig:pilot-time-b} isolates the
seven cases in which the \emph{same} operator repeated a task under both
conditions: five of seven finish faster under shared autonomy, and six of
seven travel further doing it.

separate wearer and so cannot speak to the wearer-side hypotheses that

treats extending it.
