\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

A teleoperation system for two seven-degree-of-freedom arms worn by one
person and commanded by another was built, verified, and taken to a first
pilot with real operators under ethical approval. The arms as mounted shared
no reachable point and a costed mount change fixed it; the safety layer
stopped the arm through fourteen of fourteen injected faults, and its live
record shows the manual stop firing four times while the geometric floor was
never needed; and across twenty-three sessions by five operators, shared
autonomy shortened completion and cut re-grips at the cost of a longer
robot path -- the assistance layer's, not the operators'. Nothing was validated on a loaded physical arm and the pilot had no
wearer, so the exchange rate this system exists to measure -- what
assistance buys the operator against what it costs the person carrying the
arms -- remains to be measured, and the platform, approval and input path
to measure it now exist. The habit behind every number here -- twenty-six
times an apparent system failure was an instrument defect, and establishing
which is in hand before acting -- is the one most worth carrying into that
study.
