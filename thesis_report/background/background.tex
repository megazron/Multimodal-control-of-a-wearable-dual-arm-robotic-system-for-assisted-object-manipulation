\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

\section{Supernumerary robotic limbs}

Supernumerary robotic limbs (SRLs) augment rather than restore: the wearer
keeps their own limbs and gains additional ones \cite{tong2021srlreview,
prattichizzo2021augmentation}. The design space is broad. Early systems were
oriented towards bracing and body support, in which the extra limbs take load
rather than manipulate \cite{parietti2016support}; later work added active
impedance control to improve the safety and naturalness of contact
\cite{zhang2023srlreview}. Recent reviews quantify the field's design
parameters, actuation choices, intent-recognition strategies and feedback
modalities \cite{yang2021srlreview, zheng2026quantitative}.

Two questions recur, and both bear directly on this project. The first is how
the extra limbs are \emph{commanded}, given that the wearer's own limbs are
generally occupied by the primary task. The second is whether the wearer comes
to experience the limbs as part of their own body, which is normally framed in
terms of body schema and ownership \cite{pinardi2023feedback}.

The configuration used here answers the first question by placing the
commanding person outside the wearer's body entirely, and renders the second
inapplicable: the arms are never mechanically coupled to the wearer's limbs,
so ownership language does not apply to them. The MUVE platform on which the
work was conducted mounts two Kinova Gen3 arms on a backpack frame
\cite{muve, kinova2022gen3}.

\section{Teleoperation with a separate wearer}

The direct predecessor to the two-person configuration is Fusion, which
mounted a three-axis robotic head with stereo vision and two six-degree-of-freedom
arms on a backpack worn by a \emph{surrogate}, and drove them from a remote
operator wearing a head-mounted display \cite{saraiji2018fusion}. Fusion
defined three levels of what it termed bodily driven communication ---
\emph{Direct}, \emph{Induced} and \emph{Enforced} --- distinguished by how far
the robot arms were physically coupled to the surrogate's own arms.

It is worth being precise about what those levels are, because the naming
invites a misreading. They are levels of \emph{physical coupling}, not levels
of machine autonomy: in all three the operator commanded every motion and the
machine decided nothing. Fusion was moreover presented as a two-page Emerging
Technologies demonstration and reported no user study and no quantitative
evaluation. It therefore establishes the architecture as buildable and
compelling, rather than as characterised.

The spatial behaviour of back-mounted arms around a wearer has more recently
been studied directly, with a concealed separate operator and varying apparent
autonomy \cite{zhou2026proxemics}. That work reported that higher autonomy did
\emph{not} improve the wearer's perceived safety, and produced body-zone
guidance on approach distances and trajectory shapes. It is relevant here both
as a precedent for the two-person arrangement and as a caution against
assuming that autonomy is experienced as reassuring.

\section{Master--slave teleoperation and the mapping problem}

Bilateral teleoperation, in which position and force information flow in both
directions, is the classical route to transparent interaction, and impedance
control at the follower is the usual mechanism \cite{dewolde2024impedance}.
Where the leader and follower have \emph{dissimilar} morphologies, a
joint-to-joint mapping is unavailable and the correspondence must be
established in task space, at the cost of a scaling calibration and a residual
mapping error.

Physical prototyping with miniature models is an established route to
validating such a mapping before committing to full-scale hardware, and
miniature masters have been shown to provide useful inherent haptic feedback
\cite{guggenheim2021haptic}. The device built for this project is of that
family: a scaled replica of the follower's own kinematic chain, so that the
operator's intention maps onto robot motion without an intervening
abstraction.

\section{Compliance and series elastic actuation}

Series elastic actuators place a compliant element between the motor and the
load, trading positioning bandwidth for force fidelity, shock tolerance and
safety in contact \cite{toubar2022sea, lee2019sea}. The planning report for
this project proposed a dual-layer compliance strategy: physical springs at
the miniature master and virtual spring impedance at the full-scale follower,
the latter through the manufacturer's impedance mode. \Cref{ch:discussion}
explains why the impedance layer was not delivered, and what constraint
prevented it.

\section{Teleoperation from a moving base}

Where the base of a manipulator is not inertial, base motion enters the
control problem as a disturbance. For supernumerary limbs the base is the
wearer's own body, and the disturbance is their postural sway. Zhang et
al.\ model supernumerary arms on a floating base and report tracking errors of
\SI{1.37(58)}{\milli\metre} with the human shoulder as that base
\cite{zhang2024motioncomp}.

The limitation of that result for the present work is that the wearer is also
the operator, so the disturbance is \emph{self-generated} and the person's own
motor commands are available as feed-forward. Separating the roles removes
that information entirely, which is the distinction this project is positioned
against.

\section{Simulation}

The planning report proposed Isaac Lab for offline tuning of impedance
parameters \cite{isaaclab}. The delivered work used MuJoCo for early dynamics
and ROS~2 with MoveIt~2 for the final collision-aware kinematics;
\cref{ch:discussion} gives the reasons.

\section{Research gap}

The gap the planning report identified was the absence of studies combining a
custom miniature dual-arm master with direct teleoperation of a wearable
dual-arm platform. That remains broadly true, but it requires two corrections
which this thesis makes explicitly.

First, Fusion already established the operator/wearer architecture
\cite{saraiji2018fusion}, so novelty cannot be claimed for the architecture
itself --- only for its characterisation, which Fusion did not attempt.
Second, the question of how autonomy affects the wearer is \emph{not}
untouched: \cite{zhou2026proxemics} measured exactly that and found autonomy
made perceived safety worse. What remains genuinely open is the conjunction:
no study has measured, in a system where one person wears the arms and another
drives them, whether machine autonomy changes task performance \emph{and} the
wearer's experience at the same time. Those two literatures currently point in
opposite directions, and nobody has measured both ends in one system.
